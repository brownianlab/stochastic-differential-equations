Chapter 1 — From Random Walks to Brownian Motion

Guiding question

How can a process be continuous in time, random at every scale, and still mathematically tractable enough to support calculus, pricing, and risk management?

This chapter builds Brownian motion from the discrete random walk. The point is not to introduce a mysterious object by definition, but to discover why this object is almost inevitable once one asks for a non-degenerate continuous-time limit of many small independent shocks.

We will encounter Brownian motion in several equivalent languages:

  1. as the scaling limit of random walks;
  2. as a centered Gaussian process with covariance kernel K(s,t)=min⁡(s,t)K(s,t)=\min(s,t)K(s,t)=min(s,t);
  3. as a continuous martingale with independent stationary increments;
  4. as a random element of path space;
  5. as the canonical source of quadratic variation.

These viewpoints are not decorative. Each one becomes essential later: the scaling limit explains volatility scaling, the Gaussian kernel gives geometry, the martingale property underlies no-arbitrage pricing, and quadratic variation forces Itô calculus.

1.1 Discrete randomness: the symmetric random walk​

Let (Ω,F,P)(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)(Ω,F,P) be a probability space. Let

ξ1,ξ2,…\xi_1,\xi_2,\ldotsξ1​,ξ2​,…

be independent identically distributed random variables satisfying

P(ξk=1)=P(ξk=−1)=12.\mathbb P(\xi_k=1)=\mathbb P(\xi_k=-1)=\frac12.P(ξk​=1)=P(ξk​=−1)=21​.

Define the random walk

Sn=∑k=1nξk,S0=0.S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k, \qquad S_0=0.Sn​=k=1∑n​ξk​,S0​=0.

Then

E[Sn]=0,Var⁡(Sn)=n.\mathbb E[S_n]=0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n.E[Sn​]=0,Var(Sn​)=n.
Proof

Linearity gives

E[Sn]=∑k=1nE[ξk]=0.\mathbb E[S_n]=\sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb E[\xi_k]=0.E[Sn​]=k=1∑n​E[ξk​]=0.

Since the ξk\xi_kξk​'s are independent and Var⁡(ξk)=1\operatorname{Var}(\xi_k)=1Var(ξk​)=1,

Var⁡(Sn)=∑k=1nVar⁡(ξk)=n.\operatorname{Var}(S_n) =\sum_{k=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(\xi_k) =n.Var(Sn​)=k=1∑n​Var(ξk​)=n.

The variance grows linearly in the number of steps, so the standard deviation grows like n\sqrt nn​. This square-root scaling is the first sign of Brownian geometry.

Geometric meaning of $\sqrt n$

After nnn independent zero-mean shocks, positive and negative shocks partially cancel. The net displacement is not usually of order nnn, but of order n\sqrt nn​. Randomness accumulates like Euclidean length: variances add, standard deviations scale as square roots.

1.2 Embedding the walk into continuous time​

Fix a time horizon T>0T>0T>0. For each integer nnn, divide [0,T][0,T][0,T] into nnn intervals of length

Δt=Tn.\Delta t = \frac{T}{n}.Δt=nT​.

If each shock has size Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​, define

Xt(n)=Δt S⌊t/Δt⌋.X_t^{(n)} = \sqrt{\Delta t}\, S_{\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor}.Xt(n)​=Δt​S⌊t/Δt⌋​.

At time ttt, approximately t/Δtt/\Delta tt/Δt shocks have occurred, so

Var⁡(Xt(n))=Δt ⌊tΔt⌋≈t.\operatorname{Var}\bigl(X_t^{(n)}\bigr) = \Delta t\, \left\lfloor \frac{t}{\Delta t}\right\rfloor \approx t.Var(Xt(n)​)=Δt⌊Δtt​⌋≈t.

The scaling Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ is not arbitrary. It is the only natural scaling that keeps the limiting variance finite and nonzero.

Why not scale by $\Delta t$?

If the shock size were Δt\Delta tΔt, then variance over a fixed horizon would be approximately

tΔt(Δt)2=tΔt→0.\frac{t}{\Delta t}(\Delta t)^2 = t\Delta t \to 0.Δtt​(Δt)2=tΔt→0.

The limit would become deterministic.

Why not use unscaled shocks?

If each shock had fixed size, variance after t/Δtt/\Delta tt/Δt shocks would be approximately

tΔt→∞.\frac{t}{\Delta t}\to \infty.Δtt​→∞.

The limiting object would explode.

Random walk scaling: discrete paths becoming diffusion-like
The normalization S_floor(nt)/sqrt(n) keeps variance at time t of order t. Without this scaling, paths either collapse or explode.
Finance interpretation: square-root-of-time volatility

If independent return shocks accumulate through time, variances add. Therefore volatility scales as T\sqrt TT​, not TTT. This is the primitive reason that daily volatility is annualized by multiplying by approximately 252\sqrt{252}252​, not by 252252252.

1.3 Donsker's invariance principle​

The classical central limit theorem says that for fixed time,

Snn⇒N(0,1).\frac{S_n}{\sqrt n}\Rightarrow N(0,1).n​Sn​​⇒N(0,1).

Donsker's theorem strengthens this from convergence of random variables to convergence of entire random paths.

Donsker's invariance principle, informal version

Let

Xt(n)=1nS⌊nt⌋,0≤t≤1,X_t^{(n)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}, \qquad 0\le t\le 1,Xt(n)​=n​1​S⌊nt⌋​,0≤t≤1,

with linear interpolation between grid points. Then

X(n)⇒WX^{(n)} \Rightarrow WX(n)⇒W

as random elements of the path space C([0,1])C([0,1])C([0,1]), where WWW is standard Brownian motion.

The theorem says that Brownian motion is the universal scaling limit of many small independent shocks. The detailed distribution of the shocks is not essential; under suitable moment assumptions, the same limiting process appears.

Path-space viewpoint

For each ω∈Ω\omega\in\Omegaω∈Ω, the map

t↦Xt(n)(ω)t\mapsto X_t^{(n)}(\omega)t↦Xt(n)​(ω)

is a curve. Donsker's theorem says the probability distribution on these curves converges to a probability distribution on continuous curves. Brownian motion is not one curve; it is a probability law on path space.

Brownian motion as a distribution over paths
One outcome omega gives one continuous curve t → W_t(omega). Brownian motion is the full distribution on such curves, not a single curve.

Knowing only that each WtW_tWt​ is normal does not imply that paths are continuous. Brownian motion is a process-level object. Its defining properties constrain the joint distribution of all times, not merely each isolated time.

1.5 Brownian motion as a Gaussian process​

A process (Xt)t∈I(X_t)_{t\in I}(Xt​)t∈I​ is a Gaussian process if every finite vector

(Xt1,…,Xtm)(X_{t_1},\ldots,X_{t_m})(Xt1​​,…,Xtm​​)

Brownian motion is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function

K(s,t)=Cov⁡(Ws,Wt)=min⁡(s,t).K(s,t)=\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t).K(s,t)=Cov(Ws​,Wt​)=min(s,t).

Assume s≤ts\le ts≤t. Then

Wt=Ws+(Wt−Ws).W_t = W_s + (W_t-W_s).Wt​=Ws​+(Wt​−Ws​).

By symmetry,

Cov⁡(Ws,Wt)=min⁡(s,t).\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t).Cov(Ws​,Wt​)=min(s,t).
Covariance geometry: K(s,t) = min(s,t)
Darker means larger covariance. Early time is embedded in later time: W_t = W_s + independent future increment.
Covariance as geometry

For a centered Gaussian vector, the covariance matrix determines the shape of its probability ellipsoid. For Brownian motion, the kernel K(s,t)=min⁡(s,t)K(s,t)=\min(s,t)K(s,t)=min(s,t) says that later values contain the accumulated history of earlier values. The process is not a collection of independent normal variables; it is a highly structured random curve.

1.6 Martingale property​

Let (Ft)t≥0(\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge0}(Ft​)t≥0​ be the natural filtration of Brownian motion:

Ft=σ(Ws:0≤s≤t).\mathcal F_t = \sigma(W_s:0\le s\le t).Ft​=σ(Ws​:0≤s≤t).

This represents the information generated by observing the path up to time ttt.

Brownian motion is a martingale

For 0≤s≤t0\le s\le t0≤s≤t,

E[Wt∣Fs]=Ws.\mathbb E[W_t\mid \mathcal F_s]=W_s.E[Wt​∣Fs​]=Ws​.
Proof

Write

Wt=Ws+(Wt−Ws).W_t = W_s + (W_t-W_s).Wt​=Ws​+(Wt​−Ws​).

Since WsW_sWs​ is Fs\mathcal F_sFs​-measurable,

E[Ws∣Fs]=Ws.\mathbb E[W_s\mid\mathcal F_s]=W_s.E[Ws​∣Fs​]=Ws​.

Since Wt−WsW_t-W_sWt​−Ws​ is independent of Fs\mathcal F_sFs​ and has mean zero,

E[Wt−Ws∣Fs]=0.\mathbb E[W_t-W_s\mid\mathcal F_s]=0.E[Wt​−Ws​∣Fs​]=0.

E[Wt∣Fs]=Ws.\mathbb E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=W_s.E[Wt​∣Fs​]=Ws​.
No predictable instantaneous gain

The martingale property says Brownian motion has no drift that can be predicted from its own past. In arbitrage-free pricing, discounted asset prices are martingales under a risk-neutral measure. Brownian martingales are therefore the primitive noise sources for continuous-time pricing models.

1.7 Markov property​

Brownian motion also satisfies a Markov property. Conditional on the present value WsW_sWs​, the future evolution is independent of the earlier path.

More precisely, for bounded measurable fff,

E[f(Wt)∣Fs]=E[f(Wt)∣Ws],s≤t.\mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid \mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid W_s], \qquad s\le t.E[f(Wt​)∣Fs​]=E[f(Wt​)∣Ws​],s≤t.

Since

Wt=Ws+(Wt−Ws),W_t = W_s + (W_t-W_s),Wt​=Ws​+(Wt​−Ws​),

and Wt−Ws∼N(0,t−s)W_t-W_s\sim N(0,t-s)Wt​−Ws​∼N(0,t−s), the conditional distribution of WtW_tWt​ given Ws=xW_s=xWs​=x is

Wt∣Ws=x∼N(x,t−s).W_t\mid W_s=x \sim N(x,t-s).Wt​∣Ws​=x∼N(x,t−s).
Markov geometry

The past path matters only through its endpoint. From the current point (s,Ws)(s,W_s)(s,Ws​), the future is a fresh Brownian cloud translated by WsW_sWs​. This is why Brownian transition densities satisfy heat equations.

1.9 Path roughness and non-differentiability​

For a deterministic differentiable function fff, small increments satisfy

f(t+h)−f(t)≈f′(t)h.f(t+h)-f(t)\approx f'(t)h.f(t+h)−f(t)≈f′(t)h.

The increment is order hhh. For Brownian motion,

Wt+h−Wt∼N(0,h),W_{t+h}-W_t\sim N(0,h),Wt+h​−Wt​∼N(0,h),

so typical size is order h\sqrt hh​. Therefore

Wt+h−Wth\frac{W_{t+h}-W_t}{h}hWt+h​−Wt​​

has typical magnitude of order

hh=1h→∞.\frac{\sqrt h}{h}=\frac1{\sqrt h}\to\infty.hh​​=h​1​→∞.

This heuristic explains why Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere differentiable.

Heuristic versus theorem

The above argument explains the scaling obstruction to differentiability. The rigorous theorem is stronger: Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere differentiable. Proving this requires controlling increments over countably many rational intervals and then extending to all times.

Why quadratic variation is the birth of Itô calculus

Smooth paths have increments of order Δt\Delta tΔt, so squared increments are order (Δt)2(\Delta t)^2(Δt)2, and their sum vanishes. Brownian increments are order Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​, so squared increments are order Δt\Delta tΔt, and their sum survives. This surviving second-order term is exactly why stochastic calculus differs from classical calculus.

1.12 Finance-facing interpretation​

Consider the model

dXt=μ dt+σ dWt.dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t.dXt​=μdt+σdWt​.

Over a short interval Δt\Delta tΔt, this means approximately

ΔX≈μΔt+σΔtZ,Z∼N(0,1).\Delta X\approx \mu\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}Z, \qquad Z\sim N(0,1).ΔX≈μΔt+σΔt​Z,Z∼N(0,1).

The deterministic drift is order Δt\Delta tΔt, but the random shock is order Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​. Therefore at very short time scales,

Δt≫Δt.\sqrt{\Delta t}\gg \Delta t.Δt​≫Δt.
Local randomness dominates drift

At high frequency, drift is statistically difficult to estimate because it is smaller than noise at small horizons. Volatility is locally visible; drift is not. This is one reason volatility modeling is often more statistically stable than expected-return modeling.
