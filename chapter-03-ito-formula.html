Chapter 3 — Itô's Formula

Guiding question

If fff is a smooth function and WtW_tWt​ is Brownian motion, how does f(Wt)f(W_t)f(Wt​) change over time? The ordinary chain rule says d(f∘W)=f′ dWd(f\circ W) = f'\,dWd(f∘W)=f′dW. Why is this wrong, and what replaces it?

Itô's formula is the chain rule of stochastic calculus. It is the single most important computational tool in the entire theory. Every SDE that can be solved explicitly is solved via Itô's formula. Every partial differential equation that arises in option pricing is derived via Itô's formula.

The reason the ordinary chain rule fails is the quadratic variation of Brownian motion: (dWt)2=dt(dW_t)^2 = dt(dWt​)2=dt. This creates an additional drift term proportional to the second derivative f′′f''f′′.

3.1 Why the ordinary chain rule fails​

For a deterministic differentiable function g:[0,T]→Rg:[0,T]\to\mathbb Rg:[0,T]→R and a smooth function f:R→Rf:\mathbb R\to\mathbb Rf:R→R, the chain rule gives

f(g(T))−f(g(0))=∫0Tf′(g(t)) g′(t) dt.f(g(T)) - f(g(0)) = \int_0^T f'(g(t))\,g'(t)\,dt.f(g(T))−f(g(0))=∫0T​f′(g(t))g′(t)dt.

In infinitesimal notation: d(f∘g)=f′(g) dgd(f\circ g) = f'(g)\,dgd(f∘g)=f′(g)dg. The second derivative plays no role because (dg)2∼(g′(t))2(dt)2=O(dt2)(dg)^2\sim (g'(t))^2(dt)^2 = O(dt^2)(dg)2∼(g′(t))2(dt)2=O(dt2) is negligible.

For stochastic processes, increments of order dt\sqrt{dt}dt​ change everything. Write the second-order Taylor expansion:

f(Wt+h)−f(Wt)=f′(Wt)(Wt+h−Wt)+12f′′(Wt)(Wt+h−Wt)2+O(∣Wt+h−Wt∣3).f(W_{t+h}) - f(W_t) = f'(W_t)(W_{t+h}-W_t) + \tfrac12 f''(W_t)(W_{t+h}-W_t)^2 + O(|W_{t+h}-W_t|^3).f(Wt+h​)−f(Wt​)=f′(Wt​)(Wt+h​−Wt​)+21​f′′(Wt​)(Wt+h​−Wt​)2+O(∣Wt+h​−Wt​∣3).

Summing over a partition:

f(WT)−f(W0)≈∑kf′(Wtk)ΔWk+12∑kf′′(Wtk)(ΔWk)2.f(W_T)-f(W_0) \approx \sum_k f'(W_{t_k})\Delta W_k + \tfrac12\sum_k f''(W_{t_k})(\Delta W_k)^2.f(WT​)−f(W0​)≈k∑​f′(Wtk​​)ΔWk​+21​k∑​f′′(Wtk​​)(ΔWk​)2.

As the partition refines, the first sum converges to the Itô integral ∫0Tf′(Wt) dWt\int_0^T f'(W_t)\,dW_t∫0T​f′(Wt​)dWt​. The second sum involves (ΔWk)2≈Δtk(\Delta W_k)^2\approx\Delta t_k(ΔWk​)2≈Δtk​, so it converges to 12∫0Tf′′(Wt) dt\frac12\int_0^T f''(W_t)\,dt21​∫0T​f′′(Wt​)dt.

The third-order remainder is negligible because (ΔWk)3∼(Δtk)3/2(\Delta W_k)^3 \sim (\Delta t_k)^{3/2}(ΔWk​)3∼(Δtk​)3/2 and ∑(Δtk)3/2≤∣Π∣1/2⋅T→0\sum(\Delta t_k)^{3/2} \le |\Pi|^{1/2}\cdot T\to 0∑(Δtk​)3/2≤∣Π∣1/2⋅T→0.

Why the second derivative survives

In classical calculus, the chain rule is first-order: only f′f'f′ appears because (dg)2=O(dt2)→0(dg)^2 = O(dt^2)\to 0(dg)2=O(dt2)→0.

For Brownian motion, (dW)2=O(dt)(dW)^2 = O(dt)(dW)2=O(dt), which is the same order as dtdtdt itself. So the second derivative is not negligible: it contributes a term of order dtdtdt, exactly the same magnitude as the drift.

This is the geometric content of Itô's formula: Brownian paths are so rough that second derivatives must be included in the linearization.

3.2 Itô's formula: one-dimensional, time-independent​

Itô's formula (basic version)

Let f:R→Rf:\mathbb R\to\mathbb Rf:R→R be twice continuously differentiable. Then

f(WT)−f(W0)=∫0Tf′(Wt) dWt+12∫0Tf′′(Wt) dt.f(W_T) - f(W_0) = \int_0^T f'(W_t)\,dW_t + \frac12\int_0^T f''(W_t)\,dt.f(WT​)−f(W0​)=∫0T​f′(Wt​)dWt​+21​∫0T​f′′(Wt​)dt.

In differential notation:

df(Wt)=f′(Wt) dWt+12f′′(Wt) dt.df(W_t) = f'(W_t)\,dW_t + \tfrac12 f''(W_t)\,dt.df(Wt​)=f′(Wt​)dWt​+21​f′′(Wt​)dt.
Proof via partition and Taylor expansion

Let Π={0=t0<t1<⋯<tn=T}\Pi = \{0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=T\}Π={0=t0​<t1​<⋯<tn​=T}. By the Taylor expansion to second order:

f(Wtk+1)−f(Wtk)=f′(Wtk)ΔWk+12f′′(Wtk)(ΔWk)2+Rk,f(W_{t_{k+1}})-f(W_{t_k}) = f'(W_{t_k})\Delta W_k + \tfrac12 f''(W_{t_k})(\Delta W_k)^2 + R_k,f(Wtk+1​​)−f(Wtk​​)=f′(Wtk​​)ΔWk​+21​f′′(Wtk​​)(ΔWk​)2+Rk​,

where Rk=12[f′′(ξk)−f′′(Wtk)](ΔWk)2R_k = \frac12[f''(\xi_k)-f''(W_{t_k})](\Delta W_k)^2Rk​=21​[f′′(ξk​)−f′′(Wtk​​)](ΔWk​)2 for some ξk\xi_kξk​ between WtkW_{t_k}Wtk​​ and Wtk+1W_{t_{k+1}}Wtk+1​​.

Step 1. Summing and telescoping:

f(WT)−f(W0)=∑kf′(Wtk)ΔWk+12∑kf′′(Wtk)(ΔWk)2+∑kRk.f(W_T)-f(W_0) = \sum_k f'(W_{t_k})\Delta W_k + \tfrac12\sum_k f''(W_{t_k})(\Delta W_k)^2 + \sum_k R_k.f(WT​)−f(W0​)=k∑​f′(Wtk​​)ΔWk​+21​k∑​f′′(Wtk​​)(ΔWk​)2+k∑​Rk​.

Step 2. The first sum converges in L2L^2L2 to ∫0Tf′(Wt) dWt\int_0^T f'(W_t)\,dW_t∫0T​f′(Wt​)dWt​ by definition of the Itô integral.

Step 3. For the second sum, write:

∑kf′′(Wtk)(ΔWk)2=∑kf′′(Wtk)Δtk+∑kf′′(Wtk)[(ΔWk)2−Δtk].\sum_k f''(W_{t_k})(\Delta W_k)^2 = \sum_k f''(W_{t_k})\Delta t_k + \sum_k f''(W_{t_k})[(\Delta W_k)^2-\Delta t_k].k∑​f′′(Wtk​​)(ΔWk​)2=k∑​f′′(Wtk​​)Δtk​+k∑​f′′(Wtk​​)[(ΔWk​)2−Δtk​].

The first part converges to ∫0Tf′′(Wt) dt\int_0^T f''(W_t)\,dt∫0T​f′′(Wt​)dt. For the second part, its L2L^2L2 norm vanishes as ∣Π∣→0|\Pi|\to 0∣Π∣→0 (the increments (ΔWk)2−Δtk(\Delta W_k)^2-\Delta t_k(ΔWk​)2−Δtk​ are centered and orthogonal, so their sum's variance is 2∑k(Δtk)2≤2∣Π∣⋅T→02\sum_k(\Delta t_k)^2\le 2|\Pi|\cdot T\to 02∑k​(Δtk​)2≤2∣Π∣⋅T→0).

Step 4. The remainder terms ∑kRk→0\sum_k R_k\to 0∑k​Rk​→0 in L2L^2L2 by continuity of f′′f''f′′ and the fact that ∑k(ΔWk)2→T\sum_k(\Delta W_k)^2\to T∑k​(ΔWk​)2→T.

Combining, the result holds in L2L^2L2.

Itô formula: f(W_t) decomposes into martingale + drift correction

3.4 General Itô process​

The formula extends to any Itô process, not just Brownian motion.

Itô's formula for Itô processes

Let XtX_tXt​ satisfy

dXt=bt dt+σt dWt,dX_t = b_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t,dXt​=bt​dt+σt​dWt​,

where bbb and σ\sigmaσ are adapted and satisfy integrability conditions. Let f:R→Rf:\mathbb R\to\mathbb Rf:R→R be C2C^2C2. Then

df(Xt)=f′(Xt) dXt+12f′′(Xt) d[X]t,df(X_t) = f'(X_t)\,dX_t + \tfrac12 f''(X_t)\,d[X]_t,df(Xt​)=f′(Xt​)dXt​+21​f′′(Xt​)d[X]t​,

where d[X]t=σt2 dtd[X]_t = \sigma_t^2\,dtd[X]t​=σt2​dt. Expanding:

df(Xt)=f′(Xt)bt dt+f′(Xt)σt dWt+12f′′(Xt)σt2 dt.df(X_t) = f'(X_t)b_t\,dt + f'(X_t)\sigma_t\,dW_t + \tfrac12 f''(X_t)\sigma_t^2\,dt.df(Xt​)=f′(Xt​)bt​dt+f′(Xt​)σt​dWt​+21​f′′(Xt​)σt2​dt.

3.5 Time-dependent Itô formula​

Itô's formula with time dependence

Let f=f(t,x)f = f(t,x)f=f(t,x) be C1,2C^{1,2}C1,2 (once in ttt, twice in xxx). Then

df(t,Xt)=∂tf dt+∂xf dXt+12∂xxf (dXt)2,df(t,X_t) = \partial_t f\,dt + \partial_x f\,dX_t + \tfrac12\partial_{xx}f\,(dX_t)^2,df(t,Xt​)=∂t​fdt+∂x​fdXt​+21​∂xx​f(dXt​)2,

which expands to

df(t,Xt)=[∂tf+bt ∂xf+12σt2 ∂xxf]dt+σt ∂xf dWt.df(t,X_t) = \left[\partial_t f + b_t\,\partial_x f + \tfrac12\sigma_t^2\,\partial_{xx}f\right]dt + \sigma_t\,\partial_x f\,dW_t.df(t,Xt​)=[∂t​f+bt​∂x​f+21​σt2​∂xx​f]dt+σt​∂x​fdWt​.
Three sources of change

The function f(t,Xt)f(t,X_t)f(t,Xt​) changes due to three effects:

  1. Time drift ∂tf dt\partial_t f\,dt∂t​fdt: direct time-dependence of fff.
  2. First-order XXX term ∂xf⋅dXt\partial_x f\cdot dX_t∂x​f⋅dXt​: classical chain rule contribution.
  3. Curvature correction 12∂xxf⋅σ2 dt\frac12\partial_{xx}f\cdot\sigma^2\,dt21​∂xx​f⋅σ2dt: Brownian quadratic variation creates an extra drift proportional to the second derivative. This is the purely stochastic term.

In the language of differential geometry, the second term is the gradient dotted with the velocity, and the third term is a Laplacian-like correction from the rough geometry of Brownian paths.

3.7 Geometric Brownian Motion​

The most important SDE in finance is:

dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWt,S0>0.dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t, \quad S_0 > 0.dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​,S0​>0.

Strategy: apply Itô's formula to log⁡St\log S_tlogSt​.

Let f(x)=log⁡xf(x) = \log xf(x)=logx (for x>0x > 0x>0). Then f′(x)=1/xf'(x) = 1/xf′(x)=1/x, f′′(x)=−1/x2f''(x) = -1/x^2f′′(x)=−1/x2. Itô's formula gives:

d(log⁡St)=1St dSt+12(−1St2)(dSt)2.d(\log S_t) = \frac{1}{S_t}\,dS_t + \tfrac12\left(-\frac{1}{S_t^2}\right)(dS_t)^2.d(logSt​)=St​1​dSt​+21​(−St2​1​)(dSt​)2.

Now (dSt)2=σ2St2 dt(dS_t)^2 = \sigma^2 S_t^2\,dt(dSt​)2=σ2St2​dt, so:

d(log⁡St)=μSt dt+σSt dWtSt−12σ2 dt=(μ−12σ2)dt+σ dWt.d(\log S_t) = \frac{\mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t}{S_t} - \tfrac12\sigma^2\,dt = \left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)dt + \sigma\,dW_t.d(logSt​)=St​μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​​−21​σ2dt=(μ−21​σ2)dt+σdWt​.

The right side is a Brownian motion with constant coefficients. Integrating:

log⁡St=log⁡S0+(μ−12σ2)t+σWt,\log S_t = \log S_0 + \left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t,logSt​=logS0​+(μ−21​σ2)t+σWt​,

so

St=S0exp⁡ ⁣[(μ−12σ2)t+σWt].\boxed{S_t = S_0\exp\!\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t\right].}St​=S0​exp[(μ−21​σ2)t+σWt​].​
Geometric Brownian Motion: S_t = S₀ exp((μ − σ²/2)t + σW_t)
GBM log-returns are normal: log(S_t/S_0) ~ N((μ−σ²/2)t, σ²t). Notice that higher σ widens the band but also drags the median below the mean — Jensen's inequality in action.
Log-normal returns and the volatility drag

The expected value is E[St]=S0eμt\mathbb E[S_t] = S_0 e^{\mu t}E[St​]=S0​eμt (the drift μ\muμ drives the mean). But the median of StS_tSt​ is S0e(μ−σ2/2)tS_0 e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t}S0​e(μ−σ2/2)t, which is smaller. The term −σ2/2-\sigma^2/2−σ2/2 is the volatility drag or Itô correction: randomness moves the median below the mean due to Jensen's inequality applied to the convex exponential function. Higher volatility means slower median growth, even with the same expected return.

3.9 Itô's product rule​

As a special case, for two Itô processes XtX_tXt​ and YtY_tYt​:

d(XtYt)=Xt dYt+Yt dXt+d[X,Y]t.d(X_t Y_t) = X_t\,dY_t + Y_t\,dX_t + d[X,Y]_t.d(Xt​Yt​)=Xt​dYt​+Yt​dXt​+d[X,Y]t​.

The last term is the stochastic correction. Compare with the deterministic product rule (Leibniz rule), which has no such correction.
