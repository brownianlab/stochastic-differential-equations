# Chapter 9 — Diffusions and Generators

<MotivationBox title="Guiding question">
  Given an SDE $dX = b(X)\,dt + \sigma(X)\,dW$, is there a single operator that encodes everything
  about how smooth functions evolve under $X$? How does this operator connect SDEs to partial
  differential equations?
</MotivationBox>

The infinitesimal generator $\mathcal L$ is the differential operator that describes the local behavior of a diffusion process. It packages Itô's formula into a single operator, and its study bridges stochastic analysis and classical analysis. The generator is the stochastic analogue of the derivative — it measures how fast the expected value of a function changes along the process.

---

## 9.1 The infinitesimal generator: definition

Let $X$ be the strong solution of $dX_t = b(X_t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t)\,dW_t$. For a smooth function $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ with compact support, define:

$$
\mathcal L f(x) = \lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb E^x[f(X_t)] - f(x)}{t},
$$

where $\mathbb E^x[\cdot] = \mathbb E[\cdot\mid X_0 = x]$.

By Itô's formula, the limit exists and equals:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal L f(x) = b(x)f'(x) + \tfrac12\sigma(x)^2 f''(x).}
$$

<ProofBox title="Derivation from Itô's formula">
By Itô's formula with $f\in C^2$:

$$
f(X_t) = f(x) + \int_0^t [b(X_s)f'(X_s) + \tfrac12\sigma^2(X_s)f''(X_s)]\,ds + \int_0^t\sigma(X_s)f'(X_s)\,dW_s.
$$

Taking expectations (the stochastic integral has zero expectation):

$$
\mathbb E^x[f(X_t)] = f(x) + \mathbb E^x\!\left[\int_0^t\mathcal L f(X_s)\,ds\right].
$$

Dividing by $t$ and taking $t\to 0$ (using $X_s\to x$ and continuity of $\mathcal Lf$):

$$
\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathbb E^x[f(X_t)]-f(x)}{t} = \mathcal L f(x).
$$
</ProofBox>

## 9.2 Examples of generators

**Brownian motion** ($b=0$, $\sigma=1$):

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm BM} f = \tfrac12 f''.
$$

This is $\frac12$ the Laplacian (or the heat operator in one dimension).

**Geometric Brownian Motion** ($b=\mu x$, $\sigma = \sigma x$):

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm GBM} f = \mu x f' + \tfrac12\sigma^2 x^2 f''.
$$

**Ornstein–Uhlenbeck** ($b=\kappa(\theta-x)$, $\sigma=\sigma$):

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm OU} f = \kappa(\theta-x)f' + \tfrac12\sigma^2 f''.
$$

**CIR** ($b=\kappa(\theta-x)$, $\sigma=\sigma\sqrt{x}$):

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm CIR} f = \kappa(\theta-x)f' + \tfrac12\sigma^2 x f''.
$$

## 9.3 Dynkin's formula

<TheoremBox title="Dynkin's formula">
Let $\tau$ be a stopping time with $\mathbb E^x[\tau]<\infty$ and $f\in\text{Dom}(\mathcal L)$ (the domain of the generator). Then

$$
\mathbb E^x[f(X_\tau)] = f(x) + \mathbb E^x\!\left[\int_0^\tau \mathcal L f(X_s)\,ds\right].
$$
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof via optional stopping">
The process $M_t = f(X_t) - f(x) - \int_0^t\mathcal L f(X_s)\,ds$ is a local martingale (by Itô's formula). Under appropriate integrability, the optional stopping theorem applies at $\tau$: $\mathbb E^x[M_\tau]=0$, which is Dynkin's formula.
</ProofBox>

## 9.4 Harmonic functions

A function $f$ is **$\mathcal L$-harmonic** on a domain $D$ if $\mathcal L f = 0$ on $D$.

<TheoremBox title="Mean value property for diffusions">
If $\mathcal L f = 0$ on $D$ and $\tau_D = \inf\{t: X_t\notin D\}$ is the first exit time from $D$ (with $\mathbb E^x[\tau_D]<\infty$), then

$$
f(x) = \mathbb E^x[f(X_{\tau_D})].
$$

The value at any interior point equals the expected boundary value.
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof">
By Dynkin's formula: $\mathbb E^x[f(X_{\tau_D})] = f(x) + \mathbb E^x[\int_0^{\tau_D}\mathcal L f(X_s)\,ds] = f(x)$, since $\mathcal L f = 0$.
</ProofBox>

<GeometryBox title="Diffusions average over their domain">
For ordinary Brownian motion, $\mathcal L f = \frac12f''=0$ means $f$ is linear (in 1D). The mean value property says: the value of a linear function at any point equals its average on any symmetric interval. For multidimensional BM, harmonic functions satisfy the classical mean value property of harmonic analysis.

For diffusions with drift and varying diffusion, "harmonic" is with respect to the weighted operator $b\cdot\nabla + \frac12\sigma^2\Delta$ — the natural geometry determined by the SDE.
</GeometryBox>

## 9.5 The Kolmogorov equations

The generator connects SDEs to two classical PDEs.

**Forward (Fokker–Planck) equation.** The probability density $p(t,y;0,x) = \partial_y\mathbb P^x(X_t\le y)$ satisfies the PDE (written for the $y$-variable):

$$
\partial_t p = \mathcal L^* p,
$$

where $\mathcal L^*$ is the adjoint operator:

$$
\mathcal L^* p = -\partial_y(b(y)p) + \tfrac12\partial_{yy}(\sigma^2(y)p).
$$

This describes how the probability mass flows forward in time.

**Backward (Kolmogorov backward) equation.** The function $u(t,x) = \mathbb E^x[f(X_T)\mid X_t = x]$ satisfies the PDE (written for the $x$-variable):

$$
\partial_t u + \mathcal L_x u = 0,\qquad u(T,x) = f(x).
$$

The subscript $x$ means $\mathcal L$ acts on the spatial variable $x$, with $t$ as a parameter.

<GeometryBox title="Forward vs backward: two perspectives on the same dynamics">
The forward equation tracks the evolving probability distribution of $X_t$. The backward equation tracks the conditional expectation of a functional, running time backward from the terminal condition.

In option pricing:
- Forward equation = time-evolution of the stock price density (used in local volatility calibration).
- Backward equation = the option price PDE (Black–Scholes, Feynman–Kac).

Both encode the same SDE; the difference is which variable (current state vs future payoff) is being tracked.
</GeometryBox>

## 9.6 Generators in $d$ dimensions

For the $d$-dimensional SDE $d\mathbf X = \mathbf b(\mathbf X)\,dt + \boldsymbol\sigma(\mathbf X)\,d\mathbf W$, the generator is:

$$
\mathcal L f(\mathbf x)
= \sum_i b_i(\mathbf x)\,\partial_i f(\mathbf x)
+ \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}(\sigma\sigma^\top)_{ij}(\mathbf x)\,\partial_{ij}f(\mathbf x).
$$

The matrix $A = \sigma\sigma^\top$ is the **diffusion matrix**. The generator is:

$$
\mathcal L = \mathbf b\cdot\nabla + \tfrac12\text{tr}(A\,\nabla^2).
$$

For $A = I$ and $\mathbf b = 0$: $\mathcal L = \frac12\Delta$ (half-Laplacian).

<FinanceBox title="Generator of GBM in finance">
  For GBM $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$ (risk-neutral), the generator is:

  $$\mathcal L_{\rm BS} = rS\,\partial_S + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial_{SS}.$$

  The Black–Scholes PDE is $\partial_t f + \mathcal L_{\rm BS} f = rf$, i.e.,

  $$(\partial_t + \mathcal L_{\rm BS} - r)f = 0.$$

  The operator $\mathcal L_{\rm BS} - r$ is the Black–Scholes operator: its null space consists of
  correctly priced option prices. Adding $r$ on the right reflects discounting at the risk-free rate.
</FinanceBox>

## 9.7 Problems

<ProblemBox title="Problem 1: Generator of OU">
For $dX = -\kappa X\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, compute $\mathcal L(x^2)$ and verify via Dynkin that $\mathbb E^x[X_t^2] = x^2 e^{-2\kappa t} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1-e^{-2\kappa t})$.
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 2: Harmonic function for BM">
Find all functions $f$ on $(a,b)$ with $\mathcal L_{\rm BM}f = \frac12 f'' = 0$. What does the mean value property say for BM on $(a,b)$?
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 3: CIR generator applied to bond price">
For CIR with $\mathcal L f = \kappa(\theta-x)f' + \frac12\sigma^2 xf''$, show that the function $f(x) = e^{-Bx}$ (for constant $B$) satisfies $\mathcal L f = (\kappa\theta B - \frac12\sigma^2 B^2 x + \kappa Bx - \kappa\theta B)\cdots$ — work out the full calculation as a step toward Vasicek bond pricing.
</ProblemBox>

---

## 9.8 Selected solutions

<SolutionBox title="Solution 1">
$\mathcal L(x^2) = -\kappa x\cdot 2x + \frac12\sigma^2\cdot 2 = -2\kappa x^2 + \sigma^2$.

By Dynkin: $\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E^x[X_t^2] = \mathbb E^x[\mathcal L(X_t^2)] = -2\kappa\mathbb E^x[X_t^2] + \sigma^2$.

This is an ODE $\dot v = -2\kappa v + \sigma^2$ with solution $v(t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} + (x^2 - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa})e^{-2\kappa t}$. This matches the known variance formula.
</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 2">
$f''=0$ implies $f(x) = ax + b$ (linear functions). The mean value property: for BM starting at $x\in(a,b)$, $f(x) = \mathbb E^x[f(X_{\tau_{ab}})]$, where $\tau_{ab}$ is the first exit time from $(a,b)$. For $f$ linear, the Brownian paths "average" to reach the boundary at $a$ or $b$ proportionally: $f(x) = f(a)\mathbb P^x(X_{\tau}=a) + f(b)\mathbb P^x(X_{\tau}=b)$. With $\mathbb P^x(X_\tau=b) = (x-a)/(b-a)$, this gives $f(x) = f(a)\frac{b-x}{b-a} + f(b)\frac{x-a}{b-a}$, which is consistent with linearity.
</SolutionBox>
