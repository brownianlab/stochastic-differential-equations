# Chapter 1 — From Random Walks to Brownian Motion

<MotivationBox title="Guiding question">
  How can a process be continuous in time, random at every scale, and still mathematically tractable
  enough to support calculus, pricing, and risk management?
</MotivationBox>

This chapter builds Brownian motion from the discrete random walk. The point is not to introduce a mysterious object by definition, but to discover why this object is almost inevitable once one asks for a non-degenerate continuous-time limit of many small independent shocks.

We will encounter Brownian motion in several equivalent languages:

1. as the scaling limit of random walks;
2. as a centered Gaussian process with covariance kernel $K(s,t)=\min(s,t)$;
3. as a continuous martingale with independent stationary increments;
4. as a random element of path space;
5. as the canonical source of quadratic variation.

These viewpoints are not decorative. Each one becomes essential later: the scaling limit explains volatility scaling, the Gaussian kernel gives geometry, the martingale property underlies no-arbitrage pricing, and quadratic variation forces Itô calculus.

---

## 1.1 Discrete randomness: the symmetric random walk

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$ be a probability space. Let

$$
\xi_1,\xi_2,\ldots
$$

be independent identically distributed random variables satisfying

$$
\mathbb P(\xi_k=1)=\mathbb P(\xi_k=-1)=\frac12.
$$

Define the random walk

$$
S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k, \qquad S_0=0.
$$

Then

$$
\mathbb E[S_n]=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n.
$$

<ProofBox title="Proof">
Linearity gives

$$
\mathbb E[S_n]=\sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb E[\xi_k]=0.
$$

Since the $\xi_k$'s are independent and $\operatorname{Var}(\xi_k)=1$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(S_n)
=\sum_{k=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(\xi_k)
=n.
$$

</ProofBox>

The variance grows linearly in the number of steps, so the standard deviation grows like $\sqrt n$. This square-root scaling is the first sign of Brownian geometry.

<GeometryBox title="Geometric meaning of $\sqrt n$">
  After $n$ independent zero-mean shocks, positive and negative shocks partially cancel. The net
  displacement is not usually of order $n$, but of order $\sqrt n$. Randomness accumulates like
  Euclidean length: variances add, standard deviations scale as square roots.
</GeometryBox>

## 1.2 Embedding the walk into continuous time

Fix a time horizon $T>0$. For each integer $n$, divide $[0,T]$ into $n$ intervals of length

$$
\Delta t = \frac{T}{n}.
$$

If each shock has size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, define

$$
X_t^{(n)}
= \sqrt{\Delta t}\, S_{\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor}.
$$

At time $t$, approximately $t/\Delta t$ shocks have occurred, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}\bigl(X_t^{(n)}\bigr)
= \Delta t\, \left\lfloor \frac{t}{\Delta t}\right\rfloor
\approx t.
$$

The scaling $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ is not arbitrary. It is the only natural scaling that keeps the limiting variance finite and nonzero.

<WarningBox title="Why not scale by $\Delta t$?">
If the shock size were $\Delta t$, then variance over a fixed horizon would be approximately

$$
\frac{t}{\Delta t}(\Delta t)^2 = t\Delta t \to 0.
$$

The limit would become deterministic.

</WarningBox>

<WarningBox title="Why not use unscaled shocks?">
If each shock had fixed size, variance after $t/\Delta t$ shocks would be approximately

$$
\frac{t}{\Delta t}\to \infty.
$$

The limiting object would explode.

</WarningBox>

<RandomWalkScaling />

<FinanceBox title="Finance interpretation: square-root-of-time volatility">
  If independent return shocks accumulate through time, variances add. Therefore volatility scales
  as $\sqrt T$, not $T$. This is the primitive reason that daily volatility is annualized by
  multiplying by approximately $\sqrt{252}$, not by $252$.
</FinanceBox>

## 1.3 Donsker's invariance principle

The classical central limit theorem says that for fixed time,

$$
\frac{S_n}{\sqrt n}\Rightarrow N(0,1).
$$

Donsker's theorem strengthens this from convergence of random variables to convergence of entire random paths.

<TheoremBox title="Donsker's invariance principle, informal version">
Let

$$
X_t^{(n)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}, \qquad 0\le t\le 1,
$$

with linear interpolation between grid points. Then

$$
X^{(n)} \Rightarrow W
$$

as random elements of the path space $C([0,1])$, where $W$ is standard Brownian motion.

</TheoremBox>

The theorem says that Brownian motion is the universal scaling limit of many small independent shocks. The detailed distribution of the shocks is not essential; under suitable moment assumptions, the same limiting process appears.

<GeometryBox title="Path-space viewpoint">
For each $\omega\in\Omega$, the map

$$
t\mapsto X_t^{(n)}(\omega)
$$

is a curve. Donsker's theorem says the probability distribution on these curves converges to a probability distribution on continuous curves. Brownian motion is not one curve; it is a probability law on path space.

</GeometryBox>

## 1.4 Definition of Brownian motion

A stochastic process $W=(W_t)_{t\ge0}$ is called a standard Brownian motion if:

1. $W_0=0$ almost surely.
2. For $0\le t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_m$, the increments

$$
W_{t_1}-W_{t_0},\; W_{t_2}-W_{t_1},\ldots, W_{t_m}-W_{t_{m-1}}
$$

are independent. 3. For $0\le s<t$,

$$
W_t-W_s\sim N(0,t-s).
$$

4. The sample paths $t\mapsto W_t(\omega)$ are continuous almost surely.

<WarningBox title="Continuity is a path property, not a marginal property">
  Knowing only that each $W_t$ is normal does not imply that paths are continuous. Brownian motion
  is a process-level object. Its defining properties constrain the joint distribution of all times,
  not merely each isolated time.
</WarningBox>

## 1.5 Brownian motion as a Gaussian process

A process $(X_t)_{t\in I}$ is a Gaussian process if every finite vector

$$
(X_{t_1},\ldots,X_{t_m})
$$

has a multivariate normal distribution.

Brownian motion is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function

$$
K(s,t)=\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t).
$$

<ProofBox title="Covariance derivation">
Assume $s\le t$. Then

$$
W_t = W_s + (W_t-W_s).
$$

The increment $W_t-W_s$ is independent of $W_s$, and has mean zero. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)
&=\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(W_s, W_s+(W_t-W_s)\bigr)\\
&=\operatorname{Var}(W_s)+\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t-W_s)\\
&=s+0\\
&=s.
\end{aligned}
$$

By symmetry,

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t).
$$

</ProofBox>

<CovarianceHeatmap />

<GeometryBox title="Covariance as geometry">
  For a centered Gaussian vector, the covariance matrix determines the shape of its probability
  ellipsoid. For Brownian motion, the kernel $K(s,t)=\min(s,t)$ says that later values contain the
  accumulated history of earlier values. The process is not a collection of independent normal
  variables; it is a highly structured random curve.
</GeometryBox>

## 1.6 Martingale property

Let $(\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge0}$ be the natural filtration of Brownian motion:

$$
\mathcal F_t = \sigma(W_s:0\le s\le t).
$$

This represents the information generated by observing the path up to time $t$.

<TheoremBox title="Brownian motion is a martingale">
For $0\le s\le t$,

$$
\mathbb E[W_t\mid \mathcal F_s]=W_s.
$$

</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof">
Write

$$
W_t = W_s + (W_t-W_s).
$$

Since $W_s$ is $\mathcal F_s$-measurable,

$$
\mathbb E[W_s\mid\mathcal F_s]=W_s.
$$

Since $W_t-W_s$ is independent of $\mathcal F_s$ and has mean zero,

$$
\mathbb E[W_t-W_s\mid\mathcal F_s]=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\mathbb E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=W_s.
$$

</ProofBox>

<FinanceBox title="No predictable instantaneous gain">
  The martingale property says Brownian motion has no drift that can be predicted from its own past.
  In arbitrage-free pricing, discounted asset prices are martingales under a risk-neutral measure.
  Brownian martingales are therefore the primitive noise sources for continuous-time pricing models.
</FinanceBox>

## 1.7 Markov property

Brownian motion also satisfies a Markov property. Conditional on the present value $W_s$, the future evolution is independent of the earlier path.

More precisely, for bounded measurable $f$,

$$
\mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid \mathcal F_s]
= \mathbb E[f(W_t)\mid W_s],
\qquad s\le t.
$$

Since

$$
W_t = W_s + (W_t-W_s),
$$

and $W_t-W_s\sim N(0,t-s)$, the conditional distribution of $W_t$ given $W_s=x$ is

$$
W_t\mid W_s=x \sim N(x,t-s).
$$

<GeometryBox title="Markov geometry">
  The past path matters only through its endpoint. From the current point $(s,W_s)$, the future is a
  fresh Brownian cloud translated by $W_s$. This is why Brownian transition densities satisfy heat
  equations.
</GeometryBox>

## 1.8 Scaling invariance

For $c>0$, define

$$
\widetilde W_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt c}W_{ct}.
$$

<TheoremBox title="Brownian scaling">
  The process $\widetilde W$ is again a standard Brownian motion.
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof sketch">
We check the defining properties.

First,

$$
\widetilde W_0=0.
$$

For $s<t$,

$$
\widetilde W_t-\widetilde W_s
=\frac{1}{\sqrt c}(W_{ct}-W_{cs}).
$$

Since

$$
W_{ct}-W_{cs}\sim N(0,c(t-s)),
$$

we get

$$
\widetilde W_t-\widetilde W_s\sim N(0,t-s).
$$

Independent increments and continuity are inherited from $W$.

</ProofBox>

This scaling property is not just an algebraic curiosity. It says that Brownian motion has no intrinsic time scale. Zooming in produces statistically similar roughness.

## 1.9 Path roughness and non-differentiability

For a deterministic differentiable function $f$, small increments satisfy

$$
f(t+h)-f(t)\approx f'(t)h.
$$

The increment is order $h$. For Brownian motion,

$$
W_{t+h}-W_t\sim N(0,h),
$$

so typical size is order $\sqrt h$. Therefore

$$
\frac{W_{t+h}-W_t}{h}
$$

has typical magnitude of order

$$
\frac{\sqrt h}{h}=\frac1{\sqrt h}\to\infty.
$$

This heuristic explains why Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere differentiable.

<WarningBox title="Heuristic versus theorem">
  The above argument explains the scaling obstruction to differentiability. The rigorous theorem is
  stronger: Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere differentiable. Proving this requires
  controlling increments over countably many rational intervals and then extending to all times.
</WarningBox>

## 1.10 Quadratic variation

For a partition $\Pi=\{0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=T\}$, define

$$
Q_\Pi(W)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})^2.
$$

If the mesh $|\Pi|=\max_k(t_{k+1}-t_k)$ tends to zero, then

$$
Q_\Pi(W)\to T
$$

in probability, and under suitable nested partitions, almost surely.

<QuadraticVariationDemo />

<ProofBox title="Mean and variance computation for uniform partitions">
Let $t_k=kT/n$. Then

$$
\Delta W_k = W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}\sim N(0,T/n),
$$

and increments are independent. Hence

$$
\mathbb E[(\Delta W_k)^2]=T/n.
$$

Therefore

$$
\mathbb E\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\Delta W_k)^2\right]
=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}T/n=T.
$$

For a normal random variable $Z\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Z^2)=2\sigma^4.
$$

Thus

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\Delta W_k)^2\right)
=n\cdot 2(T/n)^2
=\frac{2T^2}{n}\to0.
$$

So the quadratic variation converges to $T$ in $L^2$, hence in probability.

</ProofBox>

<GeometryBox title="Why quadratic variation is the birth of Itô calculus">
Smooth paths have increments of order $\Delta t$, so squared increments are order $(\Delta t)^2$, and their sum vanishes. Brownian increments are order $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, so squared increments are order $\Delta t$, and their sum survives. This surviving second-order term is exactly why stochastic calculus differs from classical calculus.
</GeometryBox>

## 1.11 The symbolic rule $(dW_t)^2=dt$

The expression

$$
(dW_t)^2=dt
$$

is not ordinary algebra. It is shorthand for the quadratic variation statement

$$
\sum (\Delta W_k)^2 \to T.
$$

Similarly, because Brownian increments are order $\sqrt{dt}$, one informally keeps the products

$$
(dW_t)^2\sim dt,
$$

but discards

$$
dt\,dW_t \sim dt^{3/2},
\qquad
(dt)^2\sim dt^2.
$$

This bookkeeping becomes precise in Itô's formula.

## 1.12 Finance-facing interpretation

Consider the model

$$
dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t.
$$

Over a short interval $\Delta t$, this means approximately

$$
\Delta X\approx \mu\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}Z,
\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).
$$

The deterministic drift is order $\Delta t$, but the random shock is order $\sqrt{\Delta t}$. Therefore at very short time scales,

$$
\sqrt{\Delta t}\gg \Delta t.
$$

<FinanceBox title="Local randomness dominates drift">
  At high frequency, drift is statistically difficult to estimate because it is smaller than noise
  at small horizons. Volatility is locally visible; drift is not. This is one reason volatility
  modeling is often more statistically stable than expected-return modeling.
</FinanceBox>

## 1.13 Equivalent characterizations to remember

Brownian motion may be understood as:

| Viewpoint        | Mathematical object                            | Why it matters                    |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Scaling limit    | $S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}/\sqrt n\Rightarrow W_t$ | universality, volatility scaling  |
| Gaussian process | covariance $\min(s,t)$                         | finite-dimensional laws, geometry |
| Martingale       | $\mathbb E[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=W_s$           | no-arbitrage pricing              |
| Markov process   | future depends on present                      | PDE and transition densities      |
| Rough path       | quadratic variation $[W]_t=t$                  | Itô calculus                      |

## 1.14 Problems

<ProblemBox title="Problem 1: covariance and correlation">
Let $0<s<t$. Prove

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=s,
\qquad
\operatorname{Corr}(W_s,W_t)=\sqrt{\frac{s}{t}}.
$$

</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 2: Brownian bridge preview">
Compute the conditional distribution of $W_s$ given $W_t=x$, where $0<s<t$. Interpret the conditional mean geometrically.
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 3: drifted Brownian motion">
Let

$$
X_t=x_0+\mu t+\sigma W_t.
$$

Find $\mathbb E[X_t]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_s,X_t)$.

</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 4: quadratic variation of drifted Brownian motion">
For

$$
X_t=\mu t+\sigma W_t,
$$

compute the quadratic variation $[X]_T$.

</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 5: why ordinary chain rule fails">
  Assume informally that $dW_t^2=2W_t dW_t$, as in ordinary calculus. Compare this with what Itô's
  formula will later imply. What term is missing?
</ProblemBox>

## 1.15 Selected solutions

<SolutionBox title="Solution 1">
For $s<t$, write

$$
W_t=W_s+(W_t-W_s).
$$

The second term is independent of $W_s$, so

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)
=\operatorname{Var}(W_s)=s.
$$

Since $\operatorname{Var}(W_s)=s$ and $\operatorname{Var}(W_t)=t$,

$$
\operatorname{Corr}(W_s,W_t)
=\frac{s}{\sqrt{s}\sqrt t}
=\sqrt{\frac{s}{t}}.
$$

</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 2">
The vector $(W_s,W_t)$ is jointly Gaussian with mean zero and covariance matrix

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
s & s\\
s & t
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For jointly Gaussian variables,

$$
\mathbb E[W_s\mid W_t=x]
=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)}{\operatorname{Var}(W_t)}x
=\frac{s}{t}x.
$$

The conditional variance is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(W_s\mid W_t)
=s-\frac{s^2}{t}
=\frac{s(t-s)}{t}.
$$

Thus

$$
W_s\mid W_t=x\sim N\left(\frac{s}{t}x,\frac{s(t-s)}{t}\right).
$$

Geometrically, the conditional mean is the straight line interpolating from $(0,0)$ to $(t,x)$. The remaining variance describes random fluctuation around that line.

</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 3">
We have

$$
\mathbb E[X_t]=x_0+\mu t,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\sigma^2 t.
$$

For $s\le t$, constants and deterministic drift do not affect covariance, so

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\sigma^2\min(s,t)=\sigma^2 s.
$$

</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 4">
The finite-variation drift $\mu t$ contributes zero quadratic variation. The Brownian part contributes $\sigma^2[W]_T$. Therefore

$$
[X]_T=\sigma^2T.
$$

</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 5">
Ordinary calculus would suggest no second-order correction. Itô calculus gives

$$
d(W_t^2)=2W_t\,dW_t+dt.
$$

The missing term is $dt$, arising from $(dW_t)^2=dt$. This term is the analytic signature of Brownian quadratic variation.

</SolutionBox>

## 1.16 What this chapter gives you

You should now understand Brownian motion as a single object with several mutually reinforcing representations:

- a limit of rescaled random walks;
- a centered Gaussian process with kernel $\min(s,t)$;
- a martingale and Markov process;
- a probability law on continuous paths;
- a rough path with nonzero quadratic variation.

The next chapter constructs the Itô integral

$$
\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t,
$$

which is the first step toward solving stochastic differential equations.
