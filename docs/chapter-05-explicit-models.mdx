# Chapter 5 — Explicitly Solvable Models

<MotivationBox title="Guiding question">
  Which SDEs can be solved in closed form? What techniques reduce a nonlinear SDE to a linear one, and
  what do the solutions reveal about mean-reversion, fat tails, and the geometry of financial dynamics?
</MotivationBox>

Most SDEs require numerical simulation. But a small, important family can be solved exactly using Itô's formula and algebraic tricks. These exact solutions are the calibration targets for more complex models and the benchmarks for numerical schemes.

Three canonical models appear throughout quantitative finance:

| Model | SDE | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| GBM | $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$ | Equity prices, Black–Scholes |
| Ornstein–Uhlenbeck | $dX = \kappa(\theta-X)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ | Interest rates, spreads, log-vol |
| CIR | $dX = \kappa(\theta-X)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{X}\,dW$ | Interest rates, variance |

---

## 5.1 Geometric Brownian Motion

We derived in Chapter 3 that

$$
dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t,\qquad S_0 > 0,
$$

has the explicit solution

$$
S_t = S_0\exp\!\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t\right].
$$

Let us now study this solution carefully.

**Log-normal distribution.** Since $W_t\sim N(0,t)$, we have

$$
\log S_t \sim N\!\left(\log S_0 + (\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)t,\ \sigma^2 t\right).
$$

The price $S_t$ is log-normally distributed: $S_t = e^{\text{Normal}}$.

**Mean and variance.**

$$
\mathbb E[S_t] = S_0 e^{\mu t},
\qquad
\text{Var}(S_t) = S_0^2 e^{2\mu t}(e^{\sigma^2 t}-1).
$$

**Median.**

$$
\text{Median}(S_t) = S_0 e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t},
$$

which is less than the mean for all $\sigma > 0$. This is Jensen's inequality: $\mathbb E[e^X] \ge e^{\mathbb E[X]}$ for convex functions.

<FinanceBox title="Volatility drag: why higher vol hurts median growth">
  Imagine two assets, both with $\mu = 10\%$. Asset A has $\sigma = 10\%$, Asset B has $\sigma = 40\%$.

  After $T=10$ years:
  - $\mathbb E[S_T/S_0] = e^{0.1\times 10} \approx 2.72$ (same for both)
  - Median A: $e^{(0.10-0.005)\times 10} = e^{0.95}\approx 2.59$
  - Median B: $e^{(0.10-0.08)\times 10} = e^{0.20}\approx 1.22$

  Asset B's expected value is the same, but more than half the scenarios produce less than 22% gain over 10 years. High volatility depresses the typical outcome even when the mean is unchanged. This is the practical meaning of the $-\sigma^2/2$ Itô correction.
</FinanceBox>

<GBMSimulator />

**Return autocorrelation.** Log-returns $r_k = \log(S_{t_{k+1}}/S_{t_k})$ are i.i.d. $N((\mu-\sigma^2/2)\Delta t, \sigma^2\Delta t)$. No autocorrelation, by the independence of Brownian increments.

## 5.2 The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

<TheoremBox title="OU process: explicit solution">
The SDE

$$
dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t,
\qquad X_0 = x_0,
$$

has the unique strong solution

$$
X_t = \theta + (x_0-\theta)e^{-\kappa t} + \sigma\int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)}\,dW_s.
$$
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Derivation via integrating factor">
Define $Y_t = e^{\kappa t}(X_t - \theta)$. By Itô's product rule:

$$
dY_t = \kappa e^{\kappa t}(X_t-\theta)\,dt + e^{\kappa t}dX_t.
$$

Substituting $dX_t = \kappa(\theta-X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$:

$$
dY_t = e^{\kappa t}[\kappa(X_t-\theta)\,dt + \kappa(\theta-X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t]
= \sigma e^{\kappa t}\,dW_t.
$$

Integrating: $Y_t = Y_0 + \sigma\int_0^t e^{\kappa s}\,dW_s$.

Converting back: $X_t - \theta = e^{-\kappa t}Y_t = (x_0-\theta)e^{-\kappa t} + \sigma\int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)}\,dW_s$.
</ProofBox>

**Distribution.** Since the stochastic integral is a Gaussian with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2\int_0^t e^{-2\kappa(t-s)}\,ds = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1-e^{-2\kappa t})$:

$$
X_t \sim N\!\left(\theta + (x_0-\theta)e^{-\kappa t},\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1-e^{-2\kappa t})\right).
$$

**Stationary distribution.** As $t\to\infty$, $X_t$ converges in distribution to

$$
X_\infty \sim N\!\left(\theta,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\right).
$$

This is the unique stationary distribution of the OU process.

<GeometryBox title="Mean reversion as exponential decay">
The deterministic part of the solution is $(x_0-\theta)e^{-\kappa t}$: the displacement from the long-run mean $\theta$ decays exponentially at rate $\kappa$. The half-life of deviations is $\log 2/\kappa$. The stochastic part is the accumulated noise weighted by the exponential kernel $e^{-\kappa(t-s)}$ — older noise matters less. The OU process is a memory-fading random process: its past is discounted, not forgotten.
</GeometryBox>

<OUSimulator />

**Autocovariance.** For $s\le t$:

$$
\text{Cov}(X_s,X_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}e^{-\kappa(t-s)}(1-e^{-2\kappa s}).
$$

In the stationary regime ($s\to\infty$, $t-s = \tau$ fixed):

$$
\text{Cov}(X_{s+\tau},X_s) \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}e^{-\kappa\tau}.
$$

The autocovariance decays exponentially — a characteristic signature of mean reversion.

<FinanceBox title="OU in finance: log-volatility, spreads, convenience yields">
  The OU process models quantities that fluctuate around a long-run level:
  - **Interest rates** (Vasicek model): short rate $r_t$ reverts to $\theta$
  - **Log-volatility** in rough/stochastic vol models: $\log\sigma_t$ follows OU
  - **Commodity convenience yield**: the net benefit of holding physical commodity
  - **Credit spreads**: OU on spreads captures mean reversion after credit events

  The key feature is that extreme values are self-correcting: high values have negative expected drift, low values have positive expected drift.
</FinanceBox>

## 5.3 The CIR process

The Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) process is:

$$
dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{X_t}\,dW_t,
\qquad X_0 > 0.
$$

Unlike OU, the diffusion coefficient $\sigma\sqrt{X_t}$ vanishes at $X_t = 0$, which prevents the process from going negative (under the Feller condition).

<TheoremBox title="Feller condition for positivity">
If $2\kappa\theta \ge \sigma^2$ (the Feller condition), then $X_t > 0$ for all $t$, almost surely.

If $2\kappa\theta < \sigma^2$, then $X_t$ touches zero and reflects, but the SDE remains well-posed.
</TheoremBox>

**Why the CIR diffusion coefficient matters.** In the OU process, noise is constant: $\sigma\,dW$. In CIR, noise scales as $\sigma\sqrt{X_t}\,dW$: when $X_t$ is large, noise is proportionally larger; when $X_t\to 0$, noise vanishes, and the drift $\kappa\theta > 0$ pushes the process back up.

<GeometryBox title="The Feller boundary at zero">
The boundary $\{X=0\}$ is a reflecting boundary for the CIR process when the Feller condition fails ($2\kappa\theta < \sigma^2$), or an unattainable boundary when it holds. The scaling $\sqrt{X}$ of the diffusion near zero creates a repulsion from the boundary that is precisely calibrated by the Feller condition: when the drift $\kappa\theta$ is strong enough relative to $\sigma^2$, the boundary is never reached.
</GeometryBox>

**Distribution.** The conditional distribution of $X_t$ given $X_0=x_0$ is a scaled non-central chi-squared:

$$
\frac{4\kappa}{\sigma^2(1-e^{-\kappa t})}\,X_t \sim \chi^2\!\left(\frac{4\kappa\theta}{\sigma^2},\ \lambda\right),
$$

where $\lambda = \frac{4\kappa x_0 e^{-\kappa t}}{\sigma^2(1-e^{-\kappa t})}$ is the non-centrality parameter.

**Stationary distribution.** As $t\to\infty$, $X_t$ converges to a Gamma distribution:

$$
X_\infty \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(\frac{2\kappa\theta}{\sigma^2},\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\right).
$$

Mean $= \theta$, variance $= \frac{\sigma^2\theta}{2\kappa}$.

<FinanceBox title="CIR in finance: interest rates and variance">
  **Vasicek/CIR interest rates:** The short rate $r_t$ in the CIR model stays positive (under the Feller condition), unlike Vasicek where negative rates are possible. CIR became a benchmark short-rate model for interest rate derivatives.

  **Heston stochastic volatility:** The variance process $V_t$ in the Heston model satisfies $dV = \kappa(\theta-V)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{V}\,dW^v$. The Feller condition $2\kappa\theta > \xi^2$ ensures $V_t > 0$, keeping volatility well-defined.
</FinanceBox>

## 5.4 Linear SDEs and the integrating factor method

The technique that solved the OU process generalizes. A **linear SDE** has the form:

$$
dX_t = [a(t) + b(t)X_t]\,dt + [c(t) + d(t)X_t]\,dW_t.
$$

The solution uses the stochastic integrating factor $\Phi_t$ satisfying

$$
d\Phi_t = -b(t)\Phi_t\,dt - d(t)\Phi_t\,dW_t.
$$

By Itô's formula, this exponential process has the form

$$
\Phi_t = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t b_s\,ds - \int_0^t d_s\,dW_s - \tfrac12\int_0^t d_s^2\,ds\right),
$$

and $d(\Phi_t X_t) = \Phi_t\,c(t)\,dW_t + \Phi_t\,a(t)\,dt$, which can then be integrated.

## 5.7 Selected solutions

<SolutionBox title="Solution 1">
In the stationary OU process, $X_s$ and $X_{s+\tau}$ are jointly Gaussian. Using the explicit solution:

$$
\text{Cov}(X_s, X_{s+\tau}) = e^{-\kappa\tau}\text{Var}(X_s) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}e^{-\kappa\tau}.
$$

Since $\text{Var}(X_s) = \sigma^2/(2\kappa)$ in stationarity, $\text{Corr}(X_s,X_{s+\tau}) = e^{-\kappa\tau}$.
</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 3">
$S_T = S_0 e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T + \sigma W_T}$. Then $S_T > K$ iff $\sigma W_T > \log(K/S_0) - (\mu-\sigma^2/2)T$, iff $W_T/\sqrt{T} > d_2$ where

$$
d_2 = \frac{\log(K/S_0) - (\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}.
$$

Wait, we want $S_T > K$:

$$
\mathbb P(S_T > K) = \mathbb P\!\left(\frac{W_T}{\sqrt{T}} > \frac{\log(K/S_0)-(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right) = \Phi(-d_2) = \Phi(d_2'),
$$

where $d_2' = \frac{\log(S_0/K)+(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$. Under the risk-neutral measure ($\mu = r$), this is the $N(d_2)$ in Black–Scholes.
</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 5">
$B_t = W_t - \frac{t}{T}W_T$. Clearly $B_0 = 0$. To get the SDE, use Itô's formula for $B_t = W_t - \frac{t}{T}W_T$. Note $W_T = W_T$ is fixed at maturity $T$, not $W_t$. Write instead:

$$
B_t = \int_0^t\left(1 - \frac{t}{T}\right)dW_s - \int_0^t \frac{s}{T}\,\frac{dW_s}{1} + \cdots
$$

Actually, the SDE for the Brownian bridge is derived by noting $B_T = 0$ must hold, so the drift must absorb the Brownian path. Differentiating $B_t = W_t - \frac{t}{T}W_T$:

$$
dB_t = dW_t - \frac{1}{T}W_T\,dt.
$$

Now $W_T = B_T + \frac{T}{T}W_T = W_T$, and $\frac{W_T - B_t}{T-t}$ is the conditional expected value of what the remaining path must cover. The correct SDE form is:

$$
dB_t = -\frac{B_t}{T-t}\,dt + dW_t,
$$

with the drift pulling $B_t$ toward zero at rate $\frac{1}{T-t}$, which becomes infinite as $t\to T$, forcing the bridge to hit zero.
</SolutionBox>

## 5.8 What this chapter gives you

The three canonical models encode three fundamentally different behaviors:

- **GBM**: multiplicative noise, log-normal distribution, no mean reversion, used for asset prices.
- **OU**: additive noise, Gaussian distribution, exponential mean reversion, used for rates and spreads.
- **CIR**: multiplicative noise scaled by $\sqrt{X}$, gamma stationary distribution, positivity preservation, used for variances and rates.

Each is solved by the integrating factor method, which generalizes to all linear SDEs. The next chapter introduces the change-of-measure technique — Girsanov's theorem — which is the analytical foundation of risk-neutral pricing.
