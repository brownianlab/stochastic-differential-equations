# Chapter 4 — SDE Definition and Existence

<MotivationBox title="Guiding question">
  Given a drift function $b(t,x)$ and a diffusion function $\sigma(t,x)$, does the stochastic
  differential equation $dX_t = b(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t$ have a solution? Is it unique?
  What conditions on $b$ and $\sigma$ are needed?
</MotivationBox>

An SDE is a prescription for how a process evolves in time: a deterministic drift plus a random diffusion. The existence question asks whether such a prescription is self-consistent — whether a process with the prescribed dynamics can actually be constructed. Uniqueness asks whether the dynamics determine the process completely.

---

## 4.1 The SDE as an integral equation

The shorthand $dX_t = b(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t$ is notation for the **integral equation**:

$$
X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t b(s,X_s)\,ds + \int_0^t \sigma(s,X_s)\,dW_s,
\qquad 0\le t\le T.
$$

The first integral is a Lebesgue integral (pathwise, since $b$ is a drift), and the second is an Itô integral. Finding $X$ means finding a continuous adapted process that satisfies this identity for all $t$.

<WarningBox title="The SDE is implicit">
The unknown process $X$ appears inside the integrals on the right side. This makes the SDE an implicit equation. The existence theorem constructs $X$ as the fixed point of a contraction map in the $L^2$ sense.
</WarningBox>

---

## 4.2 Strong and weak solutions

There are two notions of solution.

A **strong solution** is a process $X$ defined on the given probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$, adapted to the filtration generated by $W$, that satisfies the integral equation pathwise.

A **weak solution** is a triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P, W, X)$ — possibly a new probability space with a new Brownian motion — on which the integral equation holds.

Every strong solution is a weak solution. The converse is false in general.

<GeometryBox title="Analogy with ODEs">
For an ODE $\dot x = f(x)$, a strong solution constructs the trajectory from the given vector field $f$ and starting point. A weak solution allows changing the underlying space (redefining what "random" means) as long as the marginal dynamics are preserved.

In finance, the distinction matters for hedging: a strong solution gives a trading strategy that replicates under the original measure, while a weak solution only gives pricing without a hedging prescription.
</GeometryBox>

---

## 4.3 Lipschitz conditions

The standard hypothesis for existence and uniqueness of strong solutions is:

<TheoremBox title="Standard conditions (Lipschitz and linear growth)">
Suppose $b,\sigma:[0,T]\times\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ satisfy:

1. **Lipschitz in space:** there exists $K>0$ such that for all $t\in[0,T]$ and $x,y\in\mathbb R$,

$$
|b(t,x)-b(t,y)| + |\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)| \le K|x-y|.
$$

2. **Linear growth:** there exists $C>0$ such that

$$
|b(t,x)|^2 + |\sigma(t,x)|^2 \le C(1+x^2).
$$

Under these conditions and any $\mathcal F_0$-measurable $X_0$ with $\mathbb E[X_0^2]<\infty$, the SDE has a unique strong solution satisfying $\mathbb E[\sup_{t\le T}X_t^2]<\infty$.
</TheoremBox>

---

## 4.4 Picard iteration: constructing the solution

The proof uses the same idea as the classical Picard–Lindelöf theorem for ODEs.

Define the sequence $X^{(0)}_t = X_0$ (constant), and

$$
X^{(n+1)}_t = X_0
+ \int_0^t b(s,X^{(n)}_s)\,ds
+ \int_0^t \sigma(s,X^{(n)}_s)\,dW_s.
$$

Each $X^{(n+1)}$ is well-defined because $X^{(n)}$ is adapted and square-integrable. We show the sequence converges.

<ProofBox title="Convergence of Picard iterates">
Let $Z^{(n)}_t = X^{(n+1)}_t - X^{(n)}_t$ and $\varepsilon_n(t) = \mathbb E[\sup_{s\le t}|Z^{(n)}_s|^2]$.

By the Lipschitz condition and the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality (Section 2.9):

$$
\varepsilon_{n+1}(t)
\le C\int_0^t \varepsilon_n(s)\,ds.
$$

Starting from $\varepsilon_0(t) = \mathbb E[\sup_{s\le t}|X^{(1)}_s - X_0|^2] \le C_0 t$ (from linear growth), iterating:

$$
\varepsilon_n(t) \le \frac{C^n t^n}{n!}C_0.
$$

Therefore $\sum_n\varepsilon_n(T)^{1/2} < \infty$, and the Borel–Cantelli argument gives almost sure and $L^2$ convergence.
</ProofBox>

## 4.5 Uniqueness via Gronwall's inequality

<TheoremBox title="Pathwise uniqueness">
Under the Lipschitz condition, if $X$ and $Y$ both solve the SDE with the same initial condition and the same Brownian motion, then $X_t = Y_t$ almost surely for all $t$.
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof">
Let $Z_t = X_t - Y_t$. Then

$$
Z_t = \int_0^t [b(s,X_s)-b(s,Y_s)]\,ds + \int_0^t [\sigma(s,X_s)-\sigma(s,Y_s)]\,dW_s.
$$

By Lipschitz and the Itô isometry/BDG inequality:

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}Z_s^2\right]
\le C\int_0^t \mathbb E[Z_s^2]\,ds
\le C\int_0^t \mathbb E\!\left[\sup_{r\le s}Z_r^2\right]ds.
$$

Let $u(t) = \mathbb E[\sup_{s\le t}Z_s^2]$. Then $u(t)\le C\int_0^t u(s)\,ds$ with $u(0)=0$.

**Gronwall's inequality:** If $u(t)\le A + C\int_0^t u(s)\,ds$ with $A=0$, then $u(t)\le 0$. Therefore $u\equiv 0$, so $Z_t = 0$ a.s.
</ProofBox>

<GeometryBox title="Gronwall as an exponential estimate">
Gronwall's inequality says: if a non-negative function grows no faster than a linear fraction of its own past accumulation, then it must be zero (when starting from zero). It is the integral analogue of the comparison principle for ODEs: if $\dot u \le Cu$, then $u(t)\le u(0)e^{Ct}$. When $u(0)=0$, $u\equiv 0$.
</GeometryBox>

## 4.6 The Markov property of SDE solutions

Under the standard conditions, the strong solution of

$$
dX_t = b(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t
$$

inherits the Markov property from Brownian motion: the future evolution depends on the past only through the current value $X_t$.

More precisely, for $s\le t$ and measurable $f$:

$$
\mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid\mathcal F_s] = \mathbb E[f(X_t)\mid X_s].
$$

This follows from pathwise uniqueness and the fact that the increments $W_t - W_s$ are independent of $\mathcal F_s$.

---

## 4.7 Beyond Lipschitz: non-standard cases

The Lipschitz condition is sufficient but not necessary. Several important models fail it but still have unique solutions:

| Model | Issue | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| CIR: $dX = \kappa(\theta-X)dt + \sigma\sqrt{X}dW$ | $\sigma(x) = \sigma\sqrt{x}$ is not Lipschitz at $x=0$ | Yamada–Watanabe uniqueness theorem |
| GBM: $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$ | Lipschitz fails globally but holds locally | Local Lipschitz + linear growth suffice |
| Absolute value: $dX = |X|^\alpha dW$ | Hölder coefficient for $\alpha < 1$ | Weak solutions exist; strong uniqueness requires $\alpha\ge\frac12$ |

---

## 4.8 A worked example: verification

**Verify that $X_t = x_0 e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t}$ solves $dX = \mu X\,dt + \sigma X\,dW$.**

Apply Itô's formula to $f(t,x) = x_0 e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma x}$:

$$
\partial_t f = \left(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)f,\quad
\partial_x f = \sigma f,\quad
\partial_{xx}f = \sigma^2 f.
$$

Therefore

$$
dX_t = \left[\left(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)f + \tfrac12\sigma^2 f\right]dt + \sigma f\,dW_t
= \mu X_t\,dt + \sigma X_t\,dW_t.
$$

The SDE is satisfied. Combined with uniqueness, this is the unique strong solution.

## 4.9 Problems

<ProblemBox title="Problem 1: Lipschitz check">
Check whether $b(x) = x^2$ satisfies the Lipschitz condition on $\mathbb R$. Does it satisfy linear growth? What does this mean for global existence of $dX = X^2\,dt + dW$?
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 2: Gronwall's lemma">
Prove Gronwall's lemma: if $u:[0,T]\to[0,\infty)$ is continuous and $u(t)\le A + C\int_0^t u(s)\,ds$, then $u(t)\le A\,e^{Ct}$. (Hint: define $v(t) = e^{-Ct}(A+C\int_0^t u(s)\,ds)$ and differentiate.)
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 3: Additive noise SDE">
Find the strong solution to $dX_t = -\alpha X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$ with $X_0 = x_0$. (Hint: use the integrating factor $e^{\alpha t}$ to reduce to an exact differential.)
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 4: Pathwise uniqueness for the OU process">
Verify directly (without citing the general theorem) that any two solutions of $dX = -\alpha X\,dt + dW$ with the same $W$ and same $X_0$ are indistinguishable, by writing the difference equation and applying Gronwall.
</ProblemBox>

## 4.10 Selected solutions

<SolutionBox title="Solution 1">
$|b(x)-b(y)| = |x^2-y^2| = |x+y|\cdot|x-y|$. For large $|x|$ or $|y|$, the factor $|x+y|$ is unbounded, so Lipschitz fails globally. Also $|b(x)|^2 = x^4$ grows faster than $C(1+x^2)$, so linear growth fails. The SDE $dX = X^2\,dt + dW$ has solutions that may explode (reach $\pm\infty$) in finite time; global existence is not guaranteed.
</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 2">
Let $v(t) = e^{-Ct}[A + C\int_0^t u(s)\,ds]$. Then $v(0) = A$ and

$$
v'(t) = -Ce^{-Ct}\left[A+C\int_0^t u\right] + Ce^{-Ct}u(t)
= Ce^{-Ct}[u(t)-(A+C\int_0^t u)]
\le 0,
$$

since $u(t)\le A + C\int_0^t u$. So $v$ is decreasing, hence $v(t)\le v(0) = A$, giving $A + C\int_0^t u\le Ae^{Ct}$. Since $u(t)\le A + C\int_0^t u\le Ae^{Ct}$, Gronwall is proved.
</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 3">
Let $Y_t = e^{\alpha t}X_t$. By Itô's product rule:

$$
dY_t = \alpha e^{\alpha t}X_t\,dt + e^{\alpha t}dX_t
= \alpha e^{\alpha t}X_t\,dt + e^{\alpha t}(-\alpha X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t)
= \sigma e^{\alpha t}\,dW_t.
$$

Integrating: $Y_t = Y_0 + \sigma\int_0^t e^{\alpha s}\,dW_s = x_0 + \sigma\int_0^t e^{\alpha s}\,dW_s$. Therefore

$$
X_t = e^{-\alpha t}x_0 + \sigma\int_0^t e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\,dW_s.
$$

This is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. It is Gaussian, mean-reverting to zero with rate $\alpha$.
</SolutionBox>
