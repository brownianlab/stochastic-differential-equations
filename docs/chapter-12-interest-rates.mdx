# Chapter 12 — Interest Rate Models

<MotivationBox title="Guiding question">
  Modeling equity prices requires one SDE. Modeling the yield curve requires modeling infinitely many
  correlated rates simultaneously. How do we build self-consistent models for the entire term structure
  that remain arbitrage-free?
</MotivationBox>

Interest rate modeling is fundamentally different from equity modeling: the "underlying" is not one number but a curve — the function $T\mapsto r(t,T)$ mapping maturity to yield. Every point on this curve is a derivative of the short rate $r_t = r(t,t)$, and all must move consistently under one probability measure.

---

## 12.1 Term structure fundamentals

A **zero-coupon bond** $P(t,T)$ pays $\$1$ at time $T$. Its price at $t$ is:

$$
P(t,T) = \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-\int_t^T r_s\,ds\right)\Bigg| \mathcal F_t\right].
$$

The continuously compounded **yield** $y(t,T)$ and **forward rate** $f(t,T)$ are:

$$
y(t,T) = -\frac{\log P(t,T)}{T-t},
\qquad
f(t,T) = -\partial_T\log P(t,T).
$$

The **short rate** is $r_t = \lim_{T\to t}f(t,T) = f(t,t)$.

The entire yield curve is determined by the bond prices $\{P(t,T): T\ge t\}$.

## 12.2 The Vasicek model

<TheoremBox title="Vasicek short-rate model">
The Vasicek model assumes:

$$
dr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t.
$$

The zero-coupon bond price is $P(t,T) = A(\tau)e^{-B(\tau)r_t}$ where $\tau = T-t$:

$$
B(\tau) = \frac{1-e^{-\kappa\tau}}{\kappa},
\quad
\log A(\tau) = \left(\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\right)(B(\tau)-\tau) - \frac{\sigma^2 B(\tau)^2}{4\kappa}.
$$
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Derivation via Feynman–Kac">
By Feynman–Kac, $P(t,r) = A(\tau)e^{-B(\tau)r}$ must solve the PDE:

$$
\partial_\tau P = \kappa(\theta-r)\partial_r P + \tfrac12\sigma^2\partial_{rr}P - rP.
$$

Substituting the affine form:

$$
\dot A e^{-Br} - A\dot B r e^{-Br}
= \kappa(\theta-r)(-AB)e^{-Br} + \tfrac12\sigma^2 AB^2 e^{-Br} - rAe^{-Br}.
$$

Dividing by $Ae^{-Br}$ and collecting $r^0$ and $r^1$ terms:

- $r^0$: $\dot A/A = -\kappa\theta B + \frac12\sigma^2 B^2$
- $r^1$: $-\dot B = -1 + \kappa B$

The $r^1$ equation gives the ODE $\dot B = 1 - \kappa B$ with $B(0) = 0$, solved by $B(\tau) = (1-e^{-\kappa\tau})/\kappa$. The $r^0$ equation gives $\log A$ by integration.
</ProofBox>

<VasicekDemo />

**Properties:**
- Exact Gaussian distribution: $r_t\mid r_0\sim N(\theta + (r_0-\theta)e^{-\kappa t}, \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1-e^{-2\kappa t}))$
- Negative rates possible (limitation for low-rate environments)
- Yield curve can be normal, flat, or inverted depending on $r_0$ vs $\theta$

## 12.3 The CIR model

To avoid negative rates, Cox–Ingersoll–Ross proposed:

$$
dr_t = \kappa(\theta-r_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t.
$$

<TheoremBox title="CIR bond price">
The bond price has the same affine form $P(t,T) = A(\tau)e^{-B(\tau)r_t}$, but with:

$$
B(\tau) = \frac{2(e^{\gamma\tau}-1)}{(\gamma+\kappa)(e^{\gamma\tau}-1)+2\gamma},
$$

$$
A(\tau) = \left(\frac{2\gamma e^{(\kappa+\gamma)\tau/2}}{(\gamma+\kappa)(e^{\gamma\tau}-1)+2\gamma}\right)^{2\kappa\theta/\sigma^2},
$$

where $\gamma = \sqrt{\kappa^2+2\sigma^2}$.
</TheoremBox>

The key difference from Vasicek: the yields in CIR are always positive (when $r_0 > 0$ and the Feller condition holds), and the vol of the short rate scales with $\sqrt{r}$ rather than being constant.

## 12.4 Affine term structure models

Both Vasicek and CIR are special cases of **affine term structure models (ATSMs)**. An ATSM assumes the state vector $\mathbf X_t$ follows an affine diffusion:

$$
d\mathbf X_t = (\boldsymbol\kappa\boldsymbol\theta - K\mathbf X_t)\,dt + \Sigma(\mathbf X_t)\,d\mathbf W_t,
$$

with the short rate $r_t = \delta_0 + \boldsymbol\delta\cdot\mathbf X_t$ (affine in the state).

**Key result:** bond prices are exponential-affine: $P(t,T) = e^{A(\tau)+\mathbf B(\tau)\cdot\mathbf X_t}$, where $(A,\mathbf B)$ solve a system of Riccati ODEs.

The $A_n(m)$ classification (Dai–Singleton) organizes ATSMs by the number of state variables $n$ and the number of correlated factors driving volatility $m$.

## 12.5 The HJM framework

The Heath–Jarrow–Morton framework models the entire forward rate curve:

$$
df(t,T) = \mu(t,T)\,dt + \boldsymbol\sigma(t,T)\cdot d\mathbf W_t.
$$

Here $f(t,T)$ is the instantaneous forward rate for time $T$, observed at time $t$.

<TheoremBox title="HJM drift condition (no-arbitrage)">
Under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb Q$, the drift of $f(t,T)$ is completely determined by its volatility:

$$
\mu^{\mathbb Q}(t,T) = \boldsymbol\sigma(t,T)\cdot\int_t^T\boldsymbol\sigma(t,s)\,ds.
$$

This is the HJM drift restriction.
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof sketch">
The bond price is $P(t,T) = \exp(-\int_t^T f(t,s)\,ds)$. For $\tilde P(t,T) = e^{-rt}P(t,T)$ to be a $\mathbb Q$-martingale, Itô's formula for $P(t,T)$ (using Itô's formula for the exponential of a stochastic integral) forces the drift-volatility relationship above.
</ProofBox>

<GeometryBox title="HJM as geometry of forward rates">
The HJM condition says: in a no-arbitrage model, you cannot freely specify both the drift and the volatility of forward rates. Once you commit to a volatility structure $\sigma(t,T)$, the drift is determined. The entire arbitrage-free family of term structure models is parameterized by the volatility structure alone.

Vasicek is HJM with $\sigma(t,T) = \sigma e^{-\kappa(T-t)}$ (exponentially decaying vols). CIR is HJM with state-dependent vol. The HJM framework unifies all these as special cases.
</GeometryBox>

## 12.6 The LIBOR market model

For cap/floor pricing, the natural objects are **LIBOR forward rates** $L(t,T_i,T_{i+1})$ for a tenor grid $T_0 < T_1 < \cdots < T_n$.

In the Brace–Gatarek–Musiela (BGM)/LIBOR market model, each LIBOR rate follows a lognormal process under its own forward measure (the $T_{i+1}$-forward measure):

$$
dL_i(t) = \mu_i(t)\,dt + \sigma_i(t)\,L_i(t)\,d\tilde W_t^{T_{i+1}},
$$

where the drift $\mu_i$ (under a common measure) is determined by the terminal correlation structure and the tenor basis.

This model is consistent with Black's formula for caps and floors, making calibration straightforward.

## 12.7 Bond option pricing in Vasicek

A European call on $P(t,T_2)$ with strike $K$ and maturity $T_1 < T_2$:

$$
\text{price} = P(0,T_2)N(\hat d_1) - KP(0,T_1)N(\hat d_2),
$$

$$
\hat d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(P(0,T_2)/KP(0,T_1))}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{T_1}} \pm \frac{\hat\sigma\sqrt{T_1}}{2},
$$

$$
\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2(1-e^{-\kappa T_1})^2(1-e^{-2\kappa(T_2-T_1)})}{2\kappa^3 T_1}.
$$

This is a Black-type formula for bond options, with $\hat\sigma$ being the effective vol of the forward bond price over $[0,T_1]$.

<FinanceBox title="Negative rates and extensions">
  The Vasicek model allows negative rates, which was academic curiosity until 2014 when European
  central banks introduced negative policy rates. Gaussian models (Vasicek, Hull-White) became
  acceptable. The normal SABR model and shifted lognormal models are now standard for low-rate
  environments.

  For a Vasicek model extended to fit the initial term structure (Hull–White extension):
  $dr = (\theta(t) - \kappa r)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, where $\theta(t)$ is calibrated to match $P(0,T)$
  for all $T$. This makes the model exact for today's curve while retaining mean-reverting dynamics.
</FinanceBox>

## 12.8 Problems

<ProblemBox title="Problem 1: Vasicek yield">
Show that the Vasicek yield $y(\tau) = -\log P(\tau)/\tau$ satisfies $y(\infty) = \theta - \sigma^2/(2\kappa^2)$. Interpret: the long-run yield is the long-run rate minus a convexity adjustment.
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 2: HJM from Vasicek">
Write the Vasicek forward rate as $f(t,T) = -\partial_T\log P(t,T)$ using the affine formula, and show it follows an HJM model with $\sigma(t,T) = \sigma e^{-\kappa(T-t)}$.
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 3: Affine check">
For the two-factor Vasicek model with $\mathbf X = (r, v)$ where $dr = \kappa_r(\bar r - r)\,dt + \sigma_r\,dW^r$ and $dv = \kappa_v(\bar v - v)\,dt + \sigma_v\,dW^v$, and $r_t = r_t + v_t$, show that bond prices are still exponential-affine and write the ODEs for $A, B_r, B_v$.
</ProblemBox>

---

## 12.9 Selected solutions

<SolutionBox title="Solution 1">
As $\tau\to\infty$: $B(\tau)/\tau = (1-e^{-\kappa\tau})/(\kappa\tau)\to 0$. The log-$A$ term gives:

$$
\frac{\log A(\tau)}{\tau} \to \left(\theta-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\right)\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{B(\tau)-\tau}{\tau} - \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa}\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{B(\tau)^2}{\tau}.
$$

The second term vanishes since $B(\tau)^2/\tau\to 0$. The first term uses $B(\tau)-\tau\to -1/\kappa$... Working through the full limit gives $y(\infty) = \theta - \sigma^2/(2\kappa^2)$. The convexity adjustment $\sigma^2/(2\kappa^2)$ arises from Jensen's inequality applied to the exponential discounting.
</SolutionBox>
