# Chapter 2 — Construction of the Itô Integral

<MotivationBox title="Guiding question">
  Brownian motion is nowhere differentiable. Its total variation is almost surely infinite. Classical
  Riemann–Stieltjes integration requires the integrator to have bounded variation. How, then, can we
  define $\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t$ in a way that is mathematically precise and financially meaningful?
</MotivationBox>

The Itô integral is the central technical object of stochastic calculus. Chapter 1 built Brownian motion and established its quadratic variation property $[W]_T = T$. This chapter uses that property as a foundation. The strategy is:

1. Observe that pathwise Riemann–Stieltjes integration fails.
2. Define the integral for a small, explicitly computable class (simple processes).
3. Prove an isometry that makes the integral a bounded linear map in $L^2$.
4. Extend to the full $L^2$ class by density and closure.

The construction mirrors the standard $L^2$ completion in functional analysis, and the isometry is its counterpart of Parseval's theorem.

---

## 2.1 Why pathwise integration fails

Let $f:[0,T]\to\mathbb R$ be a continuous function and $g:[0,T]\to\mathbb R$ be the integrator. The Riemann–Stieltjes integral $\int_0^T f\,dg$ exists whenever $f$ is continuous and $g$ has **bounded variation**:

$$
\|g\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \sup_\Pi \sum_{k}|g(t_{k+1})-g(t_k)| < \infty.
$$

For Brownian motion, the opposite holds.

<TheoremBox title="Brownian motion has infinite total variation">
Almost surely,

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}|\to\infty
$$

as the mesh $|\Pi|\to 0$.
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof">
On a uniform partition with $t_k = kT/n$, the increments are i.i.d.\ $N(0,T/n)$.
Therefore

$$
\mathbb E\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}|\right]
= n\cdot \mathbb E\left[|N(0,T/n)|\right]
= n\cdot \sqrt{\frac{2T}{\pi n}}
= \sqrt{\frac{2nT}{\pi}}
\to\infty.
$$

By the law of large numbers applied to the i.i.d.\ terms $|W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}|$, the sum converges almost surely to $\infty$.
</ProofBox>

So Brownian paths are nowhere near having bounded variation. No classical integral theory applies directly.

<GeometryBox title="Why infinite variation but finite quadratic variation?">
Total variation sums $|\Delta W|$. Quadratic variation sums $(\Delta W)^2$. Because $\Delta W \sim \sqrt{\Delta t}$, we have

$$
|\Delta W| \sim \sqrt{\Delta t},
\quad
(\Delta W)^2 \sim \Delta t.
$$

Summing over $n = T/\Delta t$ terms:

$$
\sum |\Delta W| \sim n\sqrt{\Delta t} = \sqrt{T/\Delta t}\to\infty,
\quad
\sum (\Delta W)^2 \sim n\cdot\Delta t = T.
$$

Quadratic variation lives in the right scaling window to remain finite. This is why $(dW)^2 = dt$ is the key equation, and why the natural Hilbert space for stochastic integration is built on the $L^2$ norm.
</GeometryBox>

## 2.2 The class of simple adapted processes

A process $H = (H_t)_{0\le t\le T}$ is called **simple** (or elementary) if there exists a partition $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = T$ and $\mathcal F_{t_k}$-measurable random variables $H_k$ satisfying $\mathbb E[H_k^2]<\infty$, such that

$$
H_t = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k\,\mathbf{1}_{(t_k,t_{k+1}]}(t).
$$

The requirement that $H_k$ is $\mathcal F_{t_k}$-measurable is the **adaptedness** condition: the value of the integrand at time $t_k$ may only use information up to $t_k$.

<WarningBox title="Adaptedness is not optional">
If $H_k$ were allowed to depend on $W_{t_{k+1}}$ — the future increment — the resulting integral would not be a martingale, and the isometry below would fail. In finance, this corresponds to using future prices to choose a present position: pure look-ahead bias.
</WarningBox>

For a simple process, we define the Itô integral as the finite sum

$$
I(H) = \int_0^T H_t\,dW_t
:= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k\bigl(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}\bigr).
$$

Each term is a product of a $\mathcal F_{t_k}$-measurable random variable with an independent increment. This is a finite sum of square-integrable random variables, so $I(H)\in L^2(\Omega)$.

## 2.3 The Itô isometry for simple processes

<TheoremBox title="Itô isometry">
For any simple adapted process $H$,

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\!\left(\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t\right)^{\!2}\right]
= \mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt\right].
$$
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof">
Write $\Delta W_k = W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}$ and $\Delta t_k = t_{k+1}-t_k$. Then

$$
\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k\,\Delta W_k\right)^{\!2}
= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k^2\,(\Delta W_k)^2
+ 2\sum_{j<k} H_j\,\Delta W_j\,H_k\,\Delta W_k.
$$

**Cross terms.** For $j < k$, the product $H_j\,\Delta W_j\,H_k$ is $\mathcal F_{t_k}$-measurable, and $\Delta W_k$ is independent of $\mathcal F_{t_k}$ with mean zero. Therefore

$$
\mathbb E[H_j\,\Delta W_j\,H_k\,\Delta W_k]
= \mathbb E[H_j\,\Delta W_j\,H_k]\cdot\mathbb E[\Delta W_k]
= 0.
$$

**Diagonal terms.** For each $k$, $H_k$ is $\mathcal F_{t_k}$-measurable and independent of $\Delta W_k$. Therefore

$$
\mathbb E[H_k^2\,(\Delta W_k)^2]
= \mathbb E[H_k^2]\cdot\mathbb E[(\Delta W_k)^2]
= \mathbb E[H_k^2]\cdot\Delta t_k.
$$

Summing,

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t\right)^{\!2}\right]
= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb E[H_k^2]\,\Delta t_k
= \mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt\right].
$$
</ProofBox>

The cross terms vanish because of adaptedness. This is the structural insight: the left endpoint rule is exactly what kills the cross terms.

<GeometryBox title="Isometry: the integral is an isometric embedding">
The map $H\mapsto I(H)$ sends the Hilbert space

$$
L^2([0,T]\times\Omega, dt\otimes\mathbb P)
$$

isometrically into $L^2(\Omega,\mathbb P)$. Distances are preserved:

$$
\|I(H)\|_{L^2(\Omega)} = \|H\|_{L^2([0,T]\times\Omega)}.
$$

This is the stochastic counterpart of the identity $\|\hat f\|_2 = \|f\|_2$ in Fourier analysis: an integral transform that preserves $L^2$ norms.
</GeometryBox>

## 2.4 Zero mean

<TheoremBox title="Itô integral is centered">
For any simple adapted $H$,

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t\right] = 0.
$$
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof">
Each term: $\mathbb E[H_k\,\Delta W_k] = \mathbb E[H_k]\cdot\mathbb E[\Delta W_k] = 0$, since $H_k$ is independent of $\Delta W_k$ and $\mathbb E[\Delta W_k]=0$.
</ProofBox>

## 2.5 Extension to $L^2$ adapted processes

Let $\mathcal L^2 = L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)$ denote the space of adapted processes $H$ with

$$
\|H\|^2 = \mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt\right] < \infty.
$$

<TheoremBox title="Density of simple processes">
The simple adapted processes are dense in $\mathcal L^2$: for any $H\in\mathcal L^2$, there exists a sequence of simple processes $H^{(n)}$ with

$$
\|H^{(n)}-H\|_{\mathcal L^2}\to 0.
$$
</TheoremBox>

Given this density, define the Itô integral for general $H\in\mathcal L^2$ as the $L^2(\Omega)$-limit:

$$
\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t = \lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^T H_t^{(n)}\,dW_t,
$$

where the limit is in $L^2(\Omega)$.

<ProofBox title="Well-definedness">
The sequence $I(H^{(n)})$ is Cauchy in $L^2(\Omega)$ because, by the isometry,

$$
\|I(H^{(m)})-I(H^{(n)})\|_{L^2(\Omega)}
= \|I(H^{(m)}-H^{(n)})\|_{L^2(\Omega)}
= \|H^{(m)}-H^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal L^2}\to 0.
$$

Since $L^2(\Omega)$ is complete, the limit exists and is independent of the approximating sequence.
</ProofBox>

## 2.6 The Itô integral as a martingale

Define the running integral

$$
M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s,
\qquad 0\le t\le T.
$$

<TheoremBox title="Martingale property">
The process $(M_t)_{0\le t\le T}$ is a martingale with respect to the filtration $(\mathcal F_t)$.
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof sketch for simple processes">
For $s < t$ and a simple process adapted to a partition that includes $s$,

$$
M_t - M_s = \sum_{t_k\in[s,t)} H_k\,\Delta W_k.
$$

Each term $H_k\,\Delta W_k$ has mean zero conditionally on $\mathcal F_s$ (since $H_k$ is $\mathcal F_{t_k}\supseteq\mathcal F_s$-measurable and $\Delta W_k$ is independent of $\mathcal F_{t_k}$ with mean zero). Therefore

$$
\mathbb E[M_t - M_s\mid\mathcal F_s] = 0,
\qquad\text{so}\quad
\mathbb E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = M_s.
$$
</ProofBox>

<FinanceBox title="Trading P&L is a martingale under no-arbitrage">
  If $H_t$ is the number of shares of a zero-drift asset held at time $t$, and the asset price satisfies
  $dS_t = \sigma_t\,dW_t$ (a pure diffusion under the risk-neutral measure), then the portfolio value

  $$
  P_t = P_0 + \int_0^t H_s\,dS_s = P_0 + \int_0^t H_s\sigma_s\,dW_s
  $$

  is a martingale. The expected P&L is zero: there is no predictable profit without risk. This is the
  mathematical content of no-arbitrage in diffusion models.
</FinanceBox>

## 2.7 Quadratic variation of the Itô integral

<TheoremBox title="Quadratic variation">
Let $M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s$. Then the quadratic variation of $M$ is

$$
[M]_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\,ds.
$$
</TheoremBox>

<ProofBox title="Proof sketch">
On a uniform partition with mesh $\Delta t$,

$$
\sum_{k}(M_{t_{k+1}}-M_{t_k})^2
\approx \sum_k H_{t_k}^2\,(\Delta W_k)^2
\approx \sum_k H_{t_k}^2\,\Delta t
\to\int_0^t H_s^2\,ds.
$$

The approximation uses $(dW)^2 = dt$ in the same sense as the quadratic variation of Brownian motion.
</ProofBox>

<GeometryBox title="Volatility as accumulated integrand squared">
The quadratic variation $[M]_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\,ds$ is the accumulated squared magnitude of the integrand. In finance, if $H_t$ is the dollar amount in the risky asset and $dS/S = \sigma\,dW$, then the portfolio quadratic variation is $\int_0^t H_s^2\sigma_s^2\,ds$ — the accumulated squared dollar-volatility exposure.
</GeometryBox>

## 2.8 Itô versus Stratonovich

The Itô integral uses the **left endpoint** $H_k = H_{t_k}$. An alternative is the **Stratonovich integral**, which uses the **midpoint** $\frac12(H_{t_k}+H_{t_{k+1}})$:

$$
\int_0^T H_t\circ\,dW_t
:= \lim_{|\Pi|\to 0}\sum_k \frac{H_{t_k}+H_{t_{k+1}}}{2}(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}).
$$

The two integrals are related by the correction formula

$$
\int_0^T H_t\circ\,dW_t
= \int_0^T H_t\,dW_t + \tfrac12[H,W]_T,
$$

where $[H,W]_T = \int_0^T dH_s\,dW_s$ is the cross-variation.

For a smooth function $f$ applied to Brownian motion, $H_t = f(W_t)$, one computes

$$
[f(W),W]_T = \int_0^T f'(W_s)\,ds,
$$

so the Stratonovich integral satisfies the **classical chain rule**: $\int f'(W_t)\circ dW_t = f(W_T)-f(W_0)$. The Itô integral instead satisfies the modified Itô formula in Chapter 3.

<WarningBox title="Which integral to use?">
Itô: natural in finance (adapted integrands, no future look-ahead, martingale property), but its calculus has second-order correction terms.

Stratonovich: natural in physics/geometry (satisfies classical chain rule, coordinate-invariant under manifold transformations), but uses anticipating information.

In this course the default is Itô.
</WarningBox>

## 2.9 The stochastic integral as a process

For fixed $T$, the map $H\mapsto \int_0^T H_t\,dW_t$ is a random variable. But we can also view the integral as a process in the upper limit:

$$
M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s,\qquad 0\le t\le T.
$$

A version of $M$ with continuous sample paths exists whenever $H\in\mathcal L^2$.

<TheoremBox title="Continuity and the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality">
For $H\in\mathcal L^2$, the martingale $M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s$ has a continuous version. Moreover, for any $p\ge 1$, there exist universal constants $c_p, C_p$ such that

$$
c_p\,\mathbb E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\right)^{p/2}\right]
\le \mathbb E\!\left[\sup_{0\le t\le T}|M_t|^p\right]
\le C_p\,\mathbb E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\right)^{p/2}\right].
$$
</TheoremBox>

This inequality controls the supremum of the process in terms of the quadratic variation — a powerful tool in SDE existence theory.

## 2.10 A worked example

**Compute $\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t$.**

Here $H_t = W_t$. This is adapted. On a partition $\{t_k\}$, the Itô sum is

$$
I_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} W_{t_k}\,(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}).
$$

Use the algebraic identity $ab = \frac12[(a+b)^2 - a^2 - b^2]$:

$$
W_{t_k}(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})
= \tfrac12 W_{t_{k+1}}^2 - \tfrac12 W_{t_k}^2 - \tfrac12(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})^2.
$$

Summing (telescoping):

$$
I_n = \tfrac12 W_T^2 - \tfrac12 W_0^2 - \tfrac12\sum_k(\Delta W_k)^2.
$$

Since $W_0 = 0$ and $\sum_k(\Delta W_k)^2\to T$,

$$
\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t = \tfrac12 W_T^2 - \tfrac12 T.
$$

The correction $-\frac T2$ is the signature of Itô calculus. Classical calculus would give $\int_0^T x\,dx = \frac12 x^2\big|_0^T = \frac12 T$ for $x = T$, but that is the wrong analogy. The Itô integral of $W_t$ against $dW_t$ is not $\frac12 T$; it is the random variable $\frac12 W_T^2 - \frac12 T$.

## 2.11 Problems

<ProblemBox title="Problem 1: Itô isometry for a deterministic integrand">
Let $H_t = h(t)$ be a deterministic square-integrable function. Compute $\text{Var}\!\left(\int_0^T h(t)\,dW_t\right)$ using the isometry.
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 2: Integral of a constant">
Let $H_t = c$ (constant). Verify from the definition that $\int_0^T c\,dW_t = c\,W_T$, and check the Itô isometry.
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 3: Cross-variation">
Let $M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s$ and $N_t = \int_0^t K_s\,dW_s$ for $H,K\in\mathcal L^2$. Show that

$$
[M,N]_T = \int_0^T H_s K_s\,ds.
$$
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 4: The Stratonovich correction">
If $H_t = W_t$, show that the Stratonovich integral satisfies

$$
\int_0^T W_t\circ\,dW_t = \tfrac12 W_T^2.
$$

Reconcile this with $\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t = \tfrac12 W_T^2 - \tfrac12 T$ via the correction formula.
</ProblemBox>

<ProblemBox title="Problem 5: Exponential martingale">
Let $\theta\in\mathbb R$. Show that

$$
\mathcal E_t(\theta) = \exp\!\left(\theta W_t - \tfrac12\theta^2 t\right)
$$

is a martingale by verifying $d\mathcal E_t = \theta\mathcal E_t\,dW_t$ (use the Itô formula from Chapter 3 in anticipation).
</ProblemBox>

## 2.12 Selected solutions

<SolutionBox title="Solution 1">
By the Itô isometry,

$$
\text{Var}\!\left(\int_0^T h(t)\,dW_t\right)
= \mathbb E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T h(t)\,dW_t\right)^2\right]
= \int_0^T h(t)^2\,dt.
$$

The integral is also Gaussian with mean zero, so the distribution is fully determined by this variance.
</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 2">
For constant $H_t = c$, the simple-process sum is $c\sum_k\Delta W_k = c(W_T - W_0) = cW_T$. The isometry gives $\mathbb E[(cW_T)^2] = c^2 T = c^2\int_0^T dt$. Both sides match.
</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 3">
By polarization: $[M,N] = \frac14([M+N,M+N] - [M-N,M-N])$. The quadratic variation of $\int(H\pm K)\,dW$ is $\int(H\pm K)^2\,ds$. Therefore

$$
[M,N]_T = \frac14\int_0^T[(H+K)^2-(H-K)^2]\,ds = \int_0^T H_s K_s\,ds.
$$
</SolutionBox>

<SolutionBox title="Solution 4">
On each subinterval, the Stratonovich term is

$$
\frac{W_{t_k}+W_{t_{k+1}}}{2}\,\Delta W_k
= W_{t_k}\,\Delta W_k + \tfrac12(\Delta W_k)^2.
$$

Summing and passing to the limit,

$$
\int_0^T W_t\circ\,dW_t
= \int_0^T W_t\,dW_t + \tfrac12[W]_T
= \left(\tfrac12 W_T^2 - \tfrac12 T\right) + \tfrac12 T
= \tfrac12 W_T^2,
$$

which is the ordinary calculus answer.
</SolutionBox>

## 2.13 What this chapter gives you

The Itô integral $\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t$ is:

| Property | Statement |
|---|---|
| Well-defined | for $H\in L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}$ via $L^2$ completion |
| Zero mean | $\mathbb E[\int H\,dW] = 0$ |
| $L^2$ isometry | $\mathbb E[(\int H\,dW)^2] = \mathbb E[\int H^2\,dt]$ |
| Martingale | $(M_t = \int_0^t H\,dW)$ is a martingale |
| Quadratic variation | $[M]_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\,ds$ |
| Continuous paths | a continuous version of $M_t$ exists |

The next chapter derives Itô's formula, the chain rule of stochastic calculus. Itô's formula is what makes the integral computable and connects it to classical analysis and partial differential equations.
