Chapter 2 — Construction of the Itô Integral

Guiding question

Brownian motion is nowhere differentiable. Its total variation is almost surely infinite. Classical Riemann–Stieltjes integration requires the integrator to have bounded variation. How, then, can we define ∫0THt dWt\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t∫0T​Ht​dWt​ in a way that is mathematically precise and financially meaningful?

The Itô integral is the central technical object of stochastic calculus. Chapter 1 built Brownian motion and established its quadratic variation property [W]T=T[W]_T = T[W]T​=T. This chapter uses that property as a foundation. The strategy is:

  1. Observe that pathwise Riemann–Stieltjes integration fails.
  2. Define the integral for a small, explicitly computable class (simple processes).
  3. Prove an isometry that makes the integral a bounded linear map in L2L^2L2.
  4. Extend to the full L2L^2L2 class by density and closure.

The construction mirrors the standard L2L^2L2 completion in functional analysis, and the isometry is its counterpart of Parseval's theorem.

2.1 Why pathwise integration fails​

Let f:[0,T]→Rf:[0,T]\to\mathbb Rf:[0,T]→R be a continuous function and g:[0,T]→Rg:[0,T]\to\mathbb Rg:[0,T]→R be the integrator. The Riemann–Stieltjes integral ∫0Tf dg\int_0^T f\,dg∫0T​fdg exists whenever fff is continuous and ggg has bounded variation:

∥g∥TV=sup⁡Π∑k∣g(tk+1)−g(tk)∣<∞.\|g\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \sup_\Pi \sum_{k}|g(t_{k+1})-g(t_k)| < \infty.∥g∥TV​=Πsup​k∑​∣g(tk+1​)−g(tk​)∣<∞.

For Brownian motion, the opposite holds.

Brownian motion has infinite total variation

Almost surely,

∑k=0n−1∣Wtk+1−Wtk∣→∞\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}|\to\inftyk=0∑n−1​∣Wtk+1​​−Wtk​​∣→∞

as the mesh ∣Π∣→0|\Pi|\to 0∣Π∣→0.

Proof

On a uniform partition with tk=kT/nt_k = kT/ntk​=kT/n, the increments are i.i.d.\ N(0,T/n)N(0,T/n)N(0,T/n). Therefore

E[∑k=0n−1∣Wtk+1−Wtk∣]=n⋅E[∣N(0,T/n)∣]=n⋅2Tπn=2nTπ→∞.\mathbb E\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}|\right] = n\cdot \mathbb E\left[|N(0,T/n)|\right] = n\cdot \sqrt{\frac{2T}{\pi n}} = \sqrt{\frac{2nT}{\pi}} \to\infty.E[k=0∑n−1​∣Wtk+1​​−Wtk​​∣]=n⋅E[∣N(0,T/n)∣]=n⋅πn2T​​=π2nT​​→∞.

By the law of large numbers applied to the i.i.d.\ terms ∣Wtk+1−Wtk∣|W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}|∣Wtk+1​​−Wtk​​∣, the sum converges almost surely to ∞\infty∞.

So Brownian paths are nowhere near having bounded variation. No classical integral theory applies directly.

Why infinite variation but finite quadratic variation?

Total variation sums ∣ΔW∣|\Delta W|∣ΔW∣. Quadratic variation sums (ΔW)2(\Delta W)^2(ΔW)2. Because ΔW∼Δt\Delta W \sim \sqrt{\Delta t}ΔW∼Δt​, we have

∣ΔW∣∼Δt,(ΔW)2∼Δt.|\Delta W| \sim \sqrt{\Delta t}, \quad (\Delta W)^2 \sim \Delta t.∣ΔW∣∼Δt​,(ΔW)2∼Δt.

Summing over n=T/Δtn = T/\Delta tn=T/Δt terms:

∑∣ΔW∣∼nΔt=T/Δt→∞,∑(ΔW)2∼n⋅Δt=T.\sum |\Delta W| \sim n\sqrt{\Delta t} = \sqrt{T/\Delta t}\to\infty, \quad \sum (\Delta W)^2 \sim n\cdot\Delta t = T.∑∣ΔW∣∼nΔt​=T/Δt​→∞,∑(ΔW)2∼n⋅Δt=T.

Quadratic variation lives in the right scaling window to remain finite. This is why (dW)2=dt(dW)^2 = dt(dW)2=dt is the key equation, and why the natural Hilbert space for stochastic integration is built on the L2L^2L2 norm.

2.2 The class of simple adapted processes​

A process H=(Ht)0≤t≤TH = (H_t)_{0\le t\le T}H=(Ht​)0≤t≤T​ is called simple (or elementary) if there exists a partition 0=t0<t1<⋯<tn=T0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = T0=t0​<t1​<⋯<tn​=T and Ftk\mathcal F_{t_k}Ftk​​-measurable random variables HkH_kHk​ satisfying E[Hk2]<∞\mathbb E[H_k^2]<\inftyE[Hk2​]<∞, such that

Ht=∑k=0n−1Hk 1(tk,tk+1](t).H_t = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k\,\mathbf{1}_{(t_k,t_{k+1}]}(t).Ht​=k=0∑n−1​Hk​1(tk​,tk+1​]​(t).

The requirement that HkH_kHk​ is Ftk\mathcal F_{t_k}Ftk​​-measurable is the adaptedness condition: the value of the integrand at time tkt_ktk​ may only use information up to tkt_ktk​.

Adaptedness is not optional

If HkH_kHk​ were allowed to depend on Wtk+1W_{t_{k+1}}Wtk+1​​ — the future increment — the resulting integral would not be a martingale, and the isometry below would fail. In finance, this corresponds to using future prices to choose a present position: pure look-ahead bias.

For a simple process, we define the Itô integral as the finite sum

I(H)=∫0THt dWt:=∑k=0n−1Hk(Wtk+1−Wtk).I(H) = \int_0^T H_t\,dW_t := \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k\bigl(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}\bigr).I(H)=∫0T​Ht​dWt​:=k=0∑n−1​Hk​(Wtk+1​​−Wtk​​).

Each term is a product of a Ftk\mathcal F_{t_k}Ftk​​-measurable random variable with an independent increment. This is a finite sum of square-integrable random variables, so I(H)∈L2(Ω)I(H)\in L^2(\Omega)I(H)∈L2(Ω).

2.3 The Itô isometry for simple processes​

Itô isometry

For any simple adapted process HHH,

E ⁣[ ⁣(∫0THt dWt) ⁣2]=E ⁣[∫0THt2 dt].\mathbb E\!\left[\!\left(\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t\right)^{\!2}\right] = \mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt\right].E​(∫0T​Ht​dWt​)2​=E[∫0T​Ht2​dt].
Proof

Write ΔWk=Wtk+1−Wtk\Delta W_k = W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}ΔWk​=Wtk+1​​−Wtk​​ and Δtk=tk+1−tk\Delta t_k = t_{k+1}-t_kΔtk​=tk+1​−tk​. Then

(∑k=0n−1Hk ΔWk) ⁣2=∑k=0n−1Hk2 (ΔWk)2+2∑j<kHj ΔWj Hk ΔWk.\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k\,\Delta W_k\right)^{\!2} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k^2\,(\Delta W_k)^2 + 2\sum_{j<k} H_j\,\Delta W_j\,H_k\,\Delta W_k.(k=0∑n−1​Hk​ΔWk​)2=k=0∑n−1​Hk2​(ΔWk​)2+2j<k∑​Hj​ΔWj​Hk​ΔWk​.

Cross terms. For j<kj < kj<k, the product Hj ΔWj HkH_j\,\Delta W_j\,H_kHj​ΔWj​Hk​ is Ftk\mathcal F_{t_k}Ftk​​-measurable, and ΔWk\Delta W_kΔWk​ is independent of Ftk\mathcal F_{t_k}Ftk​​ with mean zero. Therefore

E[Hj ΔWj Hk ΔWk]=E[Hj ΔWj Hk]⋅E[ΔWk]=0.\mathbb E[H_j\,\Delta W_j\,H_k\,\Delta W_k] = \mathbb E[H_j\,\Delta W_j\,H_k]\cdot\mathbb E[\Delta W_k] = 0.E[Hj​ΔWj​Hk​ΔWk​]=E[Hj​ΔWj​Hk​]⋅E[ΔWk​]=0.

Diagonal terms. For each kkk, HkH_kHk​ is Ftk\mathcal F_{t_k}Ftk​​-measurable and independent of ΔWk\Delta W_kΔWk​. Therefore

E[Hk2 (ΔWk)2]=E[Hk2]⋅E[(ΔWk)2]=E[Hk2]⋅Δtk.\mathbb E[H_k^2\,(\Delta W_k)^2] = \mathbb E[H_k^2]\cdot\mathbb E[(\Delta W_k)^2] = \mathbb E[H_k^2]\cdot\Delta t_k.E[Hk2​(ΔWk​)2]=E[Hk2​]⋅E[(ΔWk​)2]=E[Hk2​]⋅Δtk​.

Summing,

E ⁣[(∫0THt dWt) ⁣2]=∑k=0n−1E[Hk2] Δtk=E ⁣[∫0THt2 dt].\mathbb E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t\right)^{\!2}\right] = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb E[H_k^2]\,\Delta t_k = \mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt\right].E​(∫0T​Ht​dWt​)2​=k=0∑n−1​E[Hk2​]Δtk​=E[∫0T​Ht2​dt].

The cross terms vanish because of adaptedness. This is the structural insight: the left endpoint rule is exactly what kills the cross terms.

Isometry: the integral is an isometric embedding

The map H↦I(H)H\mapsto I(H)H↦I(H) sends the Hilbert space

L2([0,T]×Ω,dt⊗P)L^2([0,T]\times\Omega, dt\otimes\mathbb P)L2([0,T]×Ω,dt⊗P)

isometrically into L2(Ω,P)L^2(\Omega,\mathbb P)L2(Ω,P). Distances are preserved:

∥I(H)∥L2(Ω)=∥H∥L2([0,T]×Ω).\|I(H)\|_{L^2(\Omega)} = \|H\|_{L^2([0,T]\times\Omega)}.∥I(H)∥L2(Ω)​=∥H∥L2([0,T]×Ω)​.

This is the stochastic counterpart of the identity ∥f^∥2=∥f∥2\|\hat f\|_2 = \|f\|_2∥f^​∥2​=∥f∥2​ in Fourier analysis: an integral transform that preserves L2L^2L2 norms.

2.4 Zero mean​

Itô integral is centered

For any simple adapted HHH,

E ⁣[∫0THt dWt]=0.\mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t\right] = 0.E[∫0T​Ht​dWt​]=0.
Proof

Each term: E[Hk ΔWk]=E[Hk]⋅E[ΔWk]=0\mathbb E[H_k\,\Delta W_k] = \mathbb E[H_k]\cdot\mathbb E[\Delta W_k] = 0E[Hk​ΔWk​]=E[Hk​]⋅E[ΔWk​]=0, since HkH_kHk​ is independent of ΔWk\Delta W_kΔWk​ and E[ΔWk]=0\mathbb E[\Delta W_k]=0E[ΔWk​]=0.

2.5 Extension to L2L^2L2 adapted processes​

Let L2=Lad2([0,T]×Ω)\mathcal L^2 = L^2_{\mathrm{ad}}([0,T]\times\Omega)L2=Lad2​([0,T]×Ω) denote the space of adapted processes HHH with

∥H∥2=E ⁣[∫0THt2 dt]<∞.\|H\|^2 = \mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt\right] < \infty.∥H∥2=E[∫0T​Ht2​dt]<∞.
Density of simple processes

The simple adapted processes are dense in L2\mathcal L^2L2: for any H∈L2H\in\mathcal L^2H∈L2, there exists a sequence of simple processes H(n)H^{(n)}H(n) with

∥H(n)−H∥L2→0.\|H^{(n)}-H\|_{\mathcal L^2}\to 0.∥H(n)−H∥L2​→0.

Given this density, define the Itô integral for general H∈L2H\in\mathcal L^2H∈L2 as the L2(Ω)L^2(\Omega)L2(Ω)-limit:

∫0THt dWt=lim⁡n→∞∫0THt(n) dWt,\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t = \lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^T H_t^{(n)}\,dW_t,∫0T​Ht​dWt​=n→∞lim​∫0T​Ht(n)​dWt​,

where the limit is in L2(Ω)L^2(\Omega)L2(Ω).

Well-definedness

The sequence I(H(n))I(H^{(n)})I(H(n)) is Cauchy in L2(Ω)L^2(\Omega)L2(Ω) because, by the isometry,

∥I(H(m))−I(H(n))∥L2(Ω)=∥I(H(m)−H(n))∥L2(Ω)=∥H(m)−H(n)∥L2→0.\|I(H^{(m)})-I(H^{(n)})\|_{L^2(\Omega)} = \|I(H^{(m)}-H^{(n)})\|_{L^2(\Omega)} = \|H^{(m)}-H^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal L^2}\to 0.∥I(H(m))−I(H(n))∥L2(Ω)​=∥I(H(m)−H(n))∥L2(Ω)​=∥H(m)−H(n)∥L2​→0.

Since L2(Ω)L^2(\Omega)L2(Ω) is complete, the limit exists and is independent of the approximating sequence.

2.6 The Itô integral as a martingale​

Define the running integral

Mt=∫0tHs dWs,0≤t≤T.M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s, \qquad 0\le t\le T.Mt​=∫0t​Hs​dWs​,0≤t≤T.
Martingale property

The process (Mt)0≤t≤T(M_t)_{0\le t\le T}(Mt​)0≤t≤T​ is a martingale with respect to the filtration (Ft)(\mathcal F_t)(Ft​).

Proof sketch for simple processes

For s<ts < ts<t and a simple process adapted to a partition that includes sss,

Mt−Ms=∑tk∈[s,t)Hk ΔWk.M_t - M_s = \sum_{t_k\in[s,t)} H_k\,\Delta W_k.Mt​−Ms​=tk​∈[s,t)∑​Hk​ΔWk​.

Each term Hk ΔWkH_k\,\Delta W_kHk​ΔWk​ has mean zero conditionally on Fs\mathcal F_sFs​ (since HkH_kHk​ is Ftk⊇Fs\mathcal F_{t_k}\supseteq\mathcal F_sFtk​​⊇Fs​-measurable and ΔWk\Delta W_kΔWk​ is independent of Ftk\mathcal F_{t_k}Ftk​​ with mean zero). Therefore

E[Mt−Ms∣Fs]=0,soE[Mt∣Fs]=Ms.\mathbb E[M_t - M_s\mid\mathcal F_s] = 0, \qquad\text{so}\quad \mathbb E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s] = M_s.E[Mt​−Ms​∣Fs​]=0,soE[Mt​∣Fs​]=Ms​.
Trading P&L is a martingale under no-arbitrage

If HtH_tHt​ is the number of shares of a zero-drift asset held at time ttt, and the asset price satisfies dSt=σt dWtdS_t = \sigma_t\,dW_tdSt​=σt​dWt​ (a pure diffusion under the risk-neutral measure), then the portfolio value

Pt=P0+∫0tHs dSs=P0+∫0tHsσs dWsP_t = P_0 + \int_0^t H_s\,dS_s = P_0 + \int_0^t H_s\sigma_s\,dW_sPt​=P0​+∫0t​Hs​dSs​=P0​+∫0t​Hs​σs​dWs​

is a martingale. The expected P&L is zero: there is no predictable profit without risk. This is the mathematical content of no-arbitrage in diffusion models.

2.7 Quadratic variation of the Itô integral​

Quadratic variation

Let Mt=∫0tHs dWsM_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_sMt​=∫0t​Hs​dWs​. Then the quadratic variation of MMM is

[M]t=∫0tHs2 ds.[M]_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\,ds.[M]t​=∫0t​Hs2​ds.
Proof sketch

On a uniform partition with mesh Δt\Delta tΔt,

∑k(Mtk+1−Mtk)2≈∑kHtk2 (ΔWk)2≈∑kHtk2 Δt→∫0tHs2 ds.\sum_{k}(M_{t_{k+1}}-M_{t_k})^2 \approx \sum_k H_{t_k}^2\,(\Delta W_k)^2 \approx \sum_k H_{t_k}^2\,\Delta t \to\int_0^t H_s^2\,ds.k∑​(Mtk+1​​−Mtk​​)2≈k∑​Htk​2​(ΔWk​)2≈k∑​Htk​2​Δt→∫0t​Hs2​ds.

The approximation uses (dW)2=dt(dW)^2 = dt(dW)2=dt in the same sense as the quadratic variation of Brownian motion.

Volatility as accumulated integrand squared

The quadratic variation [M]t=∫0tHs2 ds[M]_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\,ds[M]t​=∫0t​Hs2​ds is the accumulated squared magnitude of the integrand. In finance, if HtH_tHt​ is the dollar amount in the risky asset and dS/S=σ dWdS/S = \sigma\,dWdS/S=σdW, then the portfolio quadratic variation is ∫0tHs2σs2 ds\int_0^t H_s^2\sigma_s^2\,ds∫0t​Hs2​σs2​ds — the accumulated squared dollar-volatility exposure.

2.8 Itô versus Stratonovich​

The Itô integral uses the left endpoint Hk=HtkH_k = H_{t_k}Hk​=Htk​​. An alternative is the Stratonovich integral, which uses the midpoint 12(Htk+Htk+1)\frac12(H_{t_k}+H_{t_{k+1}})21​(Htk​​+Htk+1​​):

∫0THt∘ dWt:=lim⁡∣Π∣→0∑kHtk+Htk+12(Wtk+1−Wtk).\int_0^T H_t\circ\,dW_t := \lim_{|\Pi|\to 0}\sum_k \frac{H_{t_k}+H_{t_{k+1}}}{2}(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}).∫0T​Ht​∘dWt​:=∣Π∣→0lim​k∑​2Htk​​+Htk+1​​​(Wtk+1​​−Wtk​​).

The two integrals are related by the correction formula

∫0THt∘ dWt=∫0THt dWt+12[H,W]T,\int_0^T H_t\circ\,dW_t = \int_0^T H_t\,dW_t + \tfrac12[H,W]_T,∫0T​Ht​∘dWt​=∫0T​Ht​dWt​+21​[H,W]T​,

where [H,W]T=∫0TdHs dWs[H,W]_T = \int_0^T dH_s\,dW_s[H,W]T​=∫0T​dHs​dWs​ is the cross-variation.

For a smooth function fff applied to Brownian motion, Ht=f(Wt)H_t = f(W_t)Ht​=f(Wt​), one computes

[f(W),W]T=∫0Tf′(Ws) ds,[f(W),W]_T = \int_0^T f'(W_s)\,ds,[f(W),W]T​=∫0T​f′(Ws​)ds,

so the Stratonovich integral satisfies the classical chain rule: ∫f′(Wt)∘dWt=f(WT)−f(W0)\int f'(W_t)\circ dW_t = f(W_T)-f(W_0)∫f′(Wt​)∘dWt​=f(WT​)−f(W0​). The Itô integral instead satisfies the modified Itô formula in Chapter 3.

Which integral to use?

Itô: natural in finance (adapted integrands, no future look-ahead, martingale property), but its calculus has second-order correction terms.

Stratonovich: natural in physics/geometry (satisfies classical chain rule, coordinate-invariant under manifold transformations), but uses anticipating information.

In this course the default is Itô.

2.9 The stochastic integral as a process​

For fixed TTT, the map H↦∫0THt dWtH\mapsto \int_0^T H_t\,dW_tH↦∫0T​Ht​dWt​ is a random variable. But we can also view the integral as a process in the upper limit:

Mt=∫0tHs dWs,0≤t≤T.M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s,\qquad 0\le t\le T.Mt​=∫0t​Hs​dWs​,0≤t≤T.

A version of MMM with continuous sample paths exists whenever H∈L2H\in\mathcal L^2H∈L2.

Continuity and the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality

For H∈L2H\in\mathcal L^2H∈L2, the martingale Mt=∫0tHs dWsM_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_sMt​=∫0t​Hs​dWs​ has a continuous version. Moreover, for any p≥1p\ge 1p≥1, there exist universal constants cp,Cpc_p, C_pcp​,Cp​ such that

cp E ⁣[(∫0THs2 ds)p/2]≤E ⁣[sup⁡0≤t≤T∣Mt∣p]≤Cp E ⁣[(∫0THs2 ds)p/2].c_p\,\mathbb E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\right)^{p/2}\right] \le \mathbb E\!\left[\sup_{0\le t\le T}|M_t|^p\right] \le C_p\,\mathbb E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\right)^{p/2}\right].cp​E​(∫0T​Hs2​ds)p/2​≤E[0≤t≤Tsup​∣Mt​∣p]≤Cp​E​(∫0T​Hs2​ds)p/2​.

This inequality controls the supremum of the process in terms of the quadratic variation — a powerful tool in SDE existence theory.

2.11 Problems​

Problem 1: Itô isometry for a deterministic integrand

Let Ht=h(t)H_t = h(t)Ht​=h(t) be a deterministic square-integrable function. Compute Var ⁣(∫0Th(t) dWt)\text{Var}\!\left(\int_0^T h(t)\,dW_t\right)Var(∫0T​h(t)dWt​) using the isometry.

Problem 2: Integral of a constant

Let Ht=cH_t = cHt​=c (constant). Verify from the definition that ∫0Tc dWt=c WT\int_0^T c\,dW_t = c\,W_T∫0T​cdWt​=cWT​, and check the Itô isometry.

Problem 3: Cross-variation

Let Mt=∫0tHs dWsM_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_sMt​=∫0t​Hs​dWs​ and Nt=∫0tKs dWsN_t = \int_0^t K_s\,dW_sNt​=∫0t​Ks​dWs​ for H,K∈L2H,K\in\mathcal L^2H,K∈L2. Show that

[M,N]T=∫0THsKs ds.[M,N]_T = \int_0^T H_s K_s\,ds.[M,N]T​=∫0T​Hs​Ks​ds.
Problem 4: The Stratonovich correction

If Ht=WtH_t = W_tHt​=Wt​, show that the Stratonovich integral satisfies

∫0TWt∘ dWt=12WT2.\int_0^T W_t\circ\,dW_t = \tfrac12 W_T^2.∫0T​Wt​∘dWt​=21​WT2​.

Reconcile this with ∫0TWt dWt=12WT2−12T\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t = \tfrac12 W_T^2 - \tfrac12 T∫0T​Wt​dWt​=21​WT2​−21​T via the correction formula.

Problem 5: Exponential martingale

Let θ∈R\theta\in\mathbb Rθ∈R. Show that

Et(θ)=exp⁡ ⁣(θWt−12θ2t)\mathcal E_t(\theta) = \exp\!\left(\theta W_t - \tfrac12\theta^2 t\right)Et​(θ)=exp(θWt​−21​θ2t)

is a martingale by verifying dEt=θEt dWtd\mathcal E_t = \theta\mathcal E_t\,dW_tdEt​=θEt​dWt​ (use the Itô formula from Chapter 3 in anticipation).

2.12 Selected solutions​

Solution 1

By the Itô isometry,

Var ⁣(∫0Th(t) dWt)=E ⁣[(∫0Th(t) dWt)2]=∫0Th(t)2 dt.\text{Var}\!\left(\int_0^T h(t)\,dW_t\right) = \mathbb E\!\left[\left(\int_0^T h(t)\,dW_t\right)^2\right] = \int_0^T h(t)^2\,dt.Var(∫0T​h(t)dWt​)=E​(∫0T​h(t)dWt​)2​=∫0T​h(t)2dt.

The integral is also Gaussian with mean zero, so the distribution is fully determined by this variance.

Solution 2

For constant Ht=cH_t = cHt​=c, the simple-process sum is c∑kΔWk=c(WT−W0)=cWTc\sum_k\Delta W_k = c(W_T - W_0) = cW_Tc∑k​ΔWk​=c(WT​−W0​)=cWT​. The isometry gives E[(cWT)2]=c2T=c2∫0Tdt\mathbb E[(cW_T)^2] = c^2 T = c^2\int_0^T dtE[(cWT​)2]=c2T=c2∫0T​dt. Both sides match.

Solution 3

By polarization: [M,N]=14([M+N,M+N]−[M−N,M−N])[M,N] = \frac14([M+N,M+N] - [M-N,M-N])[M,N]=41​([M+N,M+N]−[M−N,M−N]). The quadratic variation of ∫(H±K) dW\int(H\pm K)\,dW∫(H±K)dW is ∫(H±K)2 ds\int(H\pm K)^2\,ds∫(H±K)2ds. Therefore

[M,N]T=14∫0T[(H+K)2−(H−K)2] ds=∫0THsKs ds.[M,N]_T = \frac14\int_0^T[(H+K)^2-(H-K)^2]\,ds = \int_0^T H_s K_s\,ds.[M,N]T​=41​∫0T​[(H+K)2−(H−K)2]ds=∫0T​Hs​Ks​ds.
Solution 4

On each subinterval, the Stratonovich term is

Wtk+Wtk+12 ΔWk=Wtk ΔWk+12(ΔWk)2.\frac{W_{t_k}+W_{t_{k+1}}}{2}\,\Delta W_k = W_{t_k}\,\Delta W_k + \tfrac12(\Delta W_k)^2.2Wtk​​+Wtk+1​​​ΔWk​=Wtk​​ΔWk​+21​(ΔWk​)2.

Summing and passing to the limit,

∫0TWt∘ dWt=∫0TWt dWt+12[W]T=(12WT2−12T)+12T=12WT2,\int_0^T W_t\circ\,dW_t = \int_0^T W_t\,dW_t + \tfrac12[W]_T = \left(\tfrac12 W_T^2 - \tfrac12 T\right) + \tfrac12 T = \tfrac12 W_T^2,∫0T​Wt​∘dWt​=∫0T​Wt​dWt​+21​[W]T​=(21​WT2​−21​T)+21​T=21​WT2​,

which is the ordinary calculus answer.
